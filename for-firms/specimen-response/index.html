What you are reading

A firm deciding where to send a United States file reasonably wants to read the work before it sends the matter. So here is the work. Below is a complete Office Action response of the kind this firm drafts, signs and files as U.S. counsel of record: the caption block, the refusal restated, the argument, the requested action, and the signature block with the certification that every paper filed at the USPTO carries.

Everything in the specimen is invented. The applicant, the mark, the cited registration and its owner, the third-party registrations, the serial and registration numbers, the exhibits, the dates and the examining attorney were written for this page and do not exist. The serial, registration and international registration numbers are taken from ranges the USPTO and WIPO have not assigned, and the law office number is not one the USPTO uses. No real matter, no real applicant and no real registration is described here.

The specimen answers a Section 2(d) likelihood-of-confusion refusal on a Madrid Protocol Section 66(a) designation, because that is the most common serious refusal in the work international firms send to U.S. counsel. Two things about it are worth saying before you read it.

A real Office Action usually raises more than one issue. The response is then a single document that answers each of them in the order the Office Action raised them, unless a different order argues better. In a Section 66(a) matter the 2(d) argument below would commonly sit alongside an amended identification of goods, since the USPTO requires wording more specific than the International Bureau accepts, and since an identification in a Section 66(a) application can be narrowed but never broadened.

A refusal grounded on a registration is a different animal from an advisory grounded on an application. Where the examining attorney points to an earlier-filed application that has not yet registered, no Section 2(d) refusal issues on it. The action carries an advisory instead, and the later-filed application is suspended to await the earlier one. TMEP § 1208.02. Which of the two you are holding changes the whole shape of the response, and it is the first thing checked when a refusal comes in.

One last note on tone. A response argues; it does not predict. Nothing in the specimen says what the examining attorney will do with it, and nothing on this page suggests what a response of this kind achieves.

The specimen response

The du Pont factors are argued below in the order this invented record supports, not in the order the case lists them. Sections that a record does not support are deleted rather than argued thinly, because a weak point dilutes a strong one.

I. Introduction

Applicant, Vasterlund Instrument AB, through its undersigned attorney of record, responds to the Office Action issued 12 January 2026 in the above application. The Office Action refused registration under Section 2(d) of the Trademark Act. For the reasons below, Applicant respectfully submits that confusion is not likely between Applicant's mark and the cited mark, and requests that the refusal be withdrawn and the application approved for publication.

II. The refusal

The Examining Attorney refused registration under Section 2(d) of the Trademark Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), on the ground that Applicant's mark VASTERLUND CLEARSPAN, for "ultrasonic thickness gauges for measuring the wall thickness of industrial pipework; hand-held ultrasonic flaw detectors for non-destructive testing of welds and metal structures" in Class 9, so resembles the mark CLEARSPAN in U.S. Registration No. 9,999,999, for "laser distance meters for construction site measurement" in Class 9, owned by Pentworth Controls Corporation, as to be likely to cause confusion, mistake, or deception. The Office Action relies on the term the marks share, on the identity of the international class, and on six third-party registrations said to show that measuring instruments of both kinds originate from a single source.

III. Applicable law

Whether confusion is likely is determined on the factors set out in In re E. I. du Pont de Nemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357 (C.C.P.A. 1973), weighed on the evidence of record. TMEP § 1207.01. Not every factor is relevant in every case, and the weight given to each varies with the record. A single factor may be dispositive, and the dissimilarity of the marks alone can be enough to find that confusion is not likely. Kellogg Co. v. Pack'em Enterprises, Inc., 951 F.2d 330 (Fed. Cir. 1991). The two factors that usually carry the most weight are the similarity of the marks and the relatedness of the goods, and both are assessed on the identifications as written rather than on the marketplace as a party describes it. Stone Lion Capital Partners, L.P. v. Lion Capital LLP, 746 F.3d 1317 (Fed. Cir. 2014).

IV. Argument

A. The marks differ in appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression (du Pont factor 1).

Marks are compared in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression. Palm Bay Imports, Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee en 1772, 396 F.3d 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2005); TMEP § 1207.01(b). The test is not whether the marks can be told apart in a side-by-side comparison, but whether their overall commercial impressions are so close that confusion as to source is likely.

1. The marks as a whole. Applicant's mark is the two-word composite VASTERLUND CLEARSPAN. The cited mark is the single term CLEARSPAN standing alone. VASTERLUND is Applicant's house mark, it is the first element a reader meets, and it has no counterpart in the cited registration. These are not the same mark with matter appended to one of them; they are a composite led by a distinctive coined term, and a one-word mark.

2. Sound. Applicant's mark is five syllables and opens on the stressed VAS, a sound the cited mark does not contain. The cited mark is two syllables. There is no correct pronunciation of a trademark, but on every likely pronunciation the marks part company at the beginning, which is ordinarily the part of a mark consumers notice and recall.

3. Appearance. Applicant's mark is nineteen letters in two words; the cited mark is nine letters in one. The cited mark is registered in standard characters and is therefore not limited to any font, size or color, so Applicant does not rest on stylization; the argument rests on the wording, which differs by the whole of its leading term.

4. Connotation. In instrumentation, span is the ordinary term for the interval between the lower and upper limits of a measuring range. Dictionary and industry-standard definitions to that effect are attached as Exhibit A. CLEARSPAN alone therefore reads, to a purchaser of measuring instruments, as a statement about the range over which an instrument reads cleanly. VASTERLUND CLEARSPAN reads as a named line of instruments from a named maker.

5. Commercial impression. The cited mark conveys a property of an instrument. Applicant's mark conveys a maker and its line. A purchaser encountering the two at different times would take the second as the product of a European instrument house named Vasterlund, not as an extension of a mark that names a measuring characteristic.

B. The shared term is weak in this field and the cited registration is entitled to a narrow scope (du Pont factor 6).

The number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods bears directly on the scope of protection the cited mark may claim. Third-party registration of a term for related goods, in sufficient number, shows that the term is weak, that consumers have learned to tell such marks apart by their other elements, and that the cited registration is entitled to a narrow scope. Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enterprises LLC, 794 F.3d 1334 (Fed. Cir. 2015); TMEP § 1207.01(d)(iii).

1. Third-party registrations. The Register contains live registrations owned by parties other than Applicant and the cited registrant for marks built on SPAN for measuring instruments. Printouts from the USPTO's TSDR database are attached as Exhibit B; a list without the printouts would be given no consideration. TMEP § 710.03. They include:

2. Consequence. The Office has repeatedly registered SPAN-formative marks for measuring instruments to unrelated owners, which shows the term understood in this field as describing what an instrument measures across rather than as pointing to one source. The shared term therefore does not carry the comparison by itself, and the elements that differ are the elements purchasers rely on. Applicant does not contend that a weak mark is unprotectable; it contends that the scope of the cited registration does not reach a composite led by a distinctive house mark.

C. The goods, their trade channels and their purchasers differ (du Pont factors 2 and 3).

The question is whether the goods, as identified, are related in a way that would lead purchasers to believe they come from one source, not whether one company could conceivably sell both. In re Coors Brewing Co., 343 F.3d 1340 (Fed. Cir. 2003); TMEP § 1207.01(a).

1. The goods as identified. Applicant's goods are ultrasonic instruments for non-destructive testing. They measure remaining wall thickness in pipework and find flaws in welds and metal structures, and they are used inside asset-integrity programs at refineries, utilities, shipyards and pipeline operators, by technicians qualified to a published non-destructive testing standard. The registrant's goods, as identified, are laser distance meters for construction site measurement: a general-purpose tool for measuring distances on a building site. Both are measuring devices in the sense that a stethoscope and a tape measure are both instruments.

2. The Examining Attorney's relatedness evidence. Of the six third-party registrations attached to the Office Action, four are owned by two large instrument houses whose Class 9 identifications sweep in every category of measuring device, and two of those four issued under Section 44(e) without any allegation of use in United States commerce. None of the six lists both non-destructive testing instruments and construction distance meters among the goods of a single registrant. Registrations of that breadth show what a large house has claimed on the Register, not what purchasers understand about the source of these two goods.

3. Trade channels. Neither identification is restricted as to channel, so the Office presumes each travels in the ordinary channels for goods of its kind, and Applicant's argument rests on the nature of the goods rather than on a restriction. Ultrasonic flaw detectors move through non-destructive testing distributors and calibration houses that serve inspection programs and that maintain traceable calibration records; construction distance meters move through builders' merchants and general tool supply.

4. Purchasers and conditions of sale (du Pont factor 4). An ultrasonic flaw detector is bought rarely, evaluated against a written procedure and a calibration requirement, and specified by qualified personnel; a construction distance meter is bought off a shelf. Purchasers of the first kind exercise care that makes source confusion with a differently named general tool unlikely. Electronic Design & Sales, Inc. v. Electronic Data Systems Corp., 954 F.2d 713 (Fed. Cir. 1992); TMEP § 1207.01(d)(vii).

V. Conclusion and requested action

Weighing the du Pont factors on this record: the marks differ in appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression; the term they share is weak in the field of measuring instruments and the cited registration is entitled to a narrow scope; and the goods as identified are bought by different purchasers, for different work, with different care. Confusion between VASTERLUND CLEARSPAN and CLEARSPAN is not likely. Applicant respectfully requests that the Section 2(d) refusal be withdrawn and that the application be approved for publication.

If any issue remains after review of this response, Applicant requests that the Examining Attorney contact the undersigned by email at nick.harris@crownhudsonlaw.com. Applicant authorizes the Examining Attorney to enter, by Examiner's Amendment issued by email, any amendment that would place the application in condition for publication.

What goes with it in a real matter

The filed response is the visible part of a matter. Five other things come with it, and they are what an instructing firm actually lives with.

A written report you can forward. Every matter closes with a report addressed to the instructing firm and written to be sent on to the holder without editing: what the refusal said, which paths were considered and why the one taken was chosen, what was filed, what the examining attorney may do next, and what is needed from your side before the next step. It is written in plain English rather than U.S. practice shorthand, because a report that has to be translated twice is not a report.

A docket entry with one date on it. The response period for a Section 66(a) matter is fixed by 37 C.F.R. § 2.62(a)(1)(ii), (a)(2) and, unlike the period in a Section 1 or Section 44 application, cannot be extended. TMEP § 711. The docket therefore carries the governing date, an earlier internal date by which the draft goes to you, and the date by which instructions must come back. You are told all three when the matter is opened, not when the first one is close.

Exhibits, assembled before the argument is written. Third-party registrations go in as TSDR printouts, not as a table; a table alone is given no consideration. TMEP § 710.03. Third-party use goes in as dated page captures showing the URL. Dictionary and standards definitions go in as printed pages. The evidence is gathered first, because the argument that survives is the one the record can carry.

Elective decisions confirmed in writing. Amending an identification, entering a disclaimer, claiming acquired distinctiveness: each changes what the holder ends up owning. Each is put to the instructing firm as a written choice with its consequences stated, confirmed by return email, and kept with the matter. Nothing that narrows a client's rights is filed on the firm's own initiative.

The attorney's own review. The certification above is a personal representation by the attorney of record, and the reading behind it is not delegable. Intake is capped at what that reading allows. When the docket is full, we say so and the matter waits or goes elsewhere.

What the firm asks for at intake

Conflicts are cleared first. After that the intake for a refusal matter is short:

  • The U.S. serial number, or a copy of the Office Action or provisional refusal as it reached you.
  • The holder's legal name, entity type and country, exactly as they should stand in the U.S. record.
  • The goods or services the holder actually cares about, in order of importance, so that any narrowing starts from the right end.
  • Whether the holder owns an earlier U.S. registration for the same or a similar mark, and whether the mark is in use in the United States.
  • Your own reading of the refusal, and any constraint carried over from the home file or the international registration.
  • Who instructs and who is invoiced, so that the engagement letter can issue naming the holder as client and your firm as the instructing firm.

Fees are flat and quoted in writing per matter type before work begins; they are set out on the pricing page. The wider arrangement, and how the client relationship stays yours, is described under U.S. counsel of record for IP firms.

The invented facts, restated

Because it matters more than once: nothing in the specimen is real. Vasterlund Instrument AB does not exist. VASTERLUND CLEARSPAN is not an application. Pentworth Controls Corporation does not exist and CLEARSPAN as described is not a registration. Serial No. 79888888, International Registration No. 9999999 and Registration Nos. 9,999,999, 9,999,101, 9,999,102 and 9,999,103 are placeholders drawn from ranges that have not been assigned, and they match no U.S. registration or application. Law Office 000 is not a USPTO law office and Marisol T. Ferncastle is not an examining attorney. The dates and the exhibits were written for this page. Exhibit B is shown with three entries to keep the page readable. A live response files the whole field, because the crowded-field authorities are distinguished about seven times in ten when the exhibit is thin, and it is the evidence rather than the citation that carries that section.

What is real is the structure, the drafting conventions and the authorities. Each authority cited in the specimen is a genuine decision or manual section, and in a live matter every one of them is pulled, read against the record and checked for currency before the response is signed. A citation that cannot be verified comes out.

If a refusal is on your desk now and you want to know what a response to it would actually argue, write to the firm with the serial number or the notification, and you will get a plain reading of the letter.
