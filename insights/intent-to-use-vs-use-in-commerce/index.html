Intent to Use vs. Use in Commerce: Choosing Your Filing Basis.

The first strategic decision in a United States trademark filing is made before a word of the application is written, and it is the one hardest to repair afterward.

By Nicholas K. Harris, Esq. · September 2026

Contents

  1. Every application stands on a basis
  2. What counts as use in commerce
  3. Intent to use, and the statement of use
  4. Timing a launch
  5. Where foreign filers go wrong

Every application stands on a basis

A federal trademark application must tell the USPTO why the applicant is entitled to file, and that reason is called the filing basis. For most applicants the intent to use vs. use in commerce question is the whole decision: is the mark already doing real work in U.S. commerce, or is that work still ahead? The answer sets the priority date, the number of steps between filing and registration, the total cost, and several of the ways an application can quietly go wrong.

The bases available under the Trademark Act:

  • Section 1(a), use in commerce. The mark is already in bona fide use in U.S. commerce for every good and service listed. The application says so under oath, states the dates of first use, and includes a specimen showing the mark at work in the market.
  • Section 1(b), intent to use. The applicant has a bona fide intention to use the mark in commerce. The application is examined and published on the same legal standards, but registration waits until use begins and is proven.
  • Section 44(e), foreign registration. An applicant holding a registration from its country of origin may register in the United States without first proving U.S. use. A bona fide intent to use is still required, and use still matters later.
  • Section 44(d), foreign priority. An applicant who filed at home first may claim that earlier date in the United States if the U.S. application follows within a short statutory window. Priority is a claim rather than a basis for registration by itself; it rides alongside Section 1(a), 1(b), or 44(e).
  • Section 66(a), the Madrid Protocol. The holder of an international registration extends protection to the United States through WIPO. The basis is fixed at filing and cannot be changed, which becomes important when things go wrong.

One rule sits above all of them: a foreign-domiciled applicant must be represented by a U.S.-licensed attorney under 37 C.F.R. 2.11, whatever the basis. The doors into the register differ; that requirement does not.

What counts as use in commerce

The Trademark Act defines use in commerce at 15 U.S.C. 1127 as the bona fide use of a mark in the ordinary course of trade, and not use made merely to reserve a right in a mark. Every clause of that definition does work, and the USPTO reads it literally.

For goods, two things must happen together. The mark must appear on the goods themselves, on their containers, tags, or labels, or on displays associated with them. And the goods must actually be sold or transported in commerce that Congress can regulate, which in practice means trade across state lines, trade between the United States and another country, or activity that substantially affects that trade. For services, the mark must be used or displayed in the sale or advertising of the services, and the services must actually be rendered in that same commerce.

What fails the definition is as instructive as what satisfies it. A single shipment arranged to create a filing date is the old token use practice that Congress abolished when it created the intent to use basis in the Trademark Law Revision Act of 1988; the modern statute was written specifically to close that door. A mark on a business plan or a pitch deck is internal, not use. A website describing a product no one can yet order is advertising a future, not selling a present. And sales made entirely in a foreign market that never touch U.S. commerce do not count at all, a point that decides more foreign filings than any other.

The proof is the specimen: a photograph of the product or its packaging, a screenshot of a working order page, marketing that shows the mark beside services genuinely on offer. Examiners read specimens skeptically and refuse the ones that look staged, and a refusal on that ground is answered the same way any refusal is, with a complete and record-supported response.

Intent to use, and the statement of use

An application under Section 1(b) requires a bona fide intention to use the mark in commerce, and bona fide is a real standard rather than a recital. When an opponent challenges intent, the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board looks for objective evidence that existed when the application was filed: a business plan, product development, manufacturing or licensing arrangements, regulatory steps. An intent formed only to warehouse a name, with nothing behind it, can void the application when someone digs.

The examination itself is identical to a use-based filing. The same refusals apply, the same requirements, the same publication for opposition. What changes is the ending. Instead of a registration, a cleared intent to use application receives a Notice of Allowance, and the last move belongs to the applicant: a Statement of Use, sworn, with a specimen and a government fee, filed within a fixed statutory window that runs from the allowance date. Extensions of that window are available in set increments, each on a written request, a fee, and a sworn statement of continued bona fide intent, with good cause required after the first, up to an outer limit the statute itself fixes. If the window closes with neither a statement nor an extension on file, the application goes abandoned and everything behind it is lost.

Use that begins early can be claimed early. Before the application is approved for publication, an Amendment to Allege Use converts the filing to a use basis midstream. There is also a blackout stretch, running from approval for publication until the Notice of Allowance issues, during which neither an Amendment to Allege Use nor a Statement of Use is accepted, one of several procedural corners where bad timing costs real money.

The Statement of Use is not a formality. It is examined too, specimen included, and a defective one draws a refusal at the very end of the process, when the applicant has the least room left to maneuver.

Timing a launch

The reason intent to use exists, and the reason careful filers reach for it, is Section 7(c) of the Act: the filing date of an application that matures to registration is treated as a date of constructive use, nationwide. Priority is fixed the day the application is filed, potentially long before the product ships, and everyone who adopts a confusingly similar mark afterward stands behind it. That one provision produces most of the practical advice.

File before the announcement, not after. A public launch, a crowdfunding page, a trade show reveal each put the name in front of competitors before any right exists. An intent to use application filed quietly beforehand locks the priority date while the project is still private.

File on use when the use is real. If the goods are genuinely selling in U.S. commerce, Section 1(a) is the shorter and cheaper road: one examination, no allowance stage, no statement of use, no extension fees.

Do not manufacture use to skip a step. The temptation is one arranged sale and a use-based filing. The dates of first use are sworn and become part of the public record, and a registration built on use that was not bona fide is exposed to attack precisely when the registration matters, in a dispute against an opponent whose lawyers look for exactly this.

A rebrand is an intent to use case. The old mark is in use; the new one is not, however finished the design work feels. Filing the new mark on intent before printers, distributors, and partners ever see it is what constructive use is for.

The economics deserve plain statement. An intent to use filing adds a stage and its government fees, and every extension adds more, so the basis is a budgeting decision as well as a legal one. Where foreign filers go wrong

Basis mistakes cluster among foreign applicants, partly because the American concept of use has no counterpart in most first-to-file systems, and partly because a generation of low-cost filing agencies taught habits the USPTO is now punishing.

Claiming use that is not U.S. use. Sales at home, however substantial, are not use in commerce under 15 U.S.C. 1127. An application filed under Section 1(a) for goods on which the mark was not in use in U.S. commerce is defective from the start. During examination the basis can sometimes be rescued by amendment to intent to use; a registration that issues on a use claim that was false is far harder to defend, and the sworn declaration behind it follows the file.

Specimens that show a mockup rather than a market. Digitally rendered packaging, a product page that does not ship to the United States, an image edited to add the mark. The USPTO has spent recent years auditing exactly this, terminating fraudulent applications by the tens of thousands and sanctioning the practitioners who signed them, and examiners now hunt the telltales as routine.

Reading Section 44(e) as proof that use never matters. A 44(e) registration issues without evidence of U.S. use, but the statute still demands a bona fide intent to use, and the registration must later be maintained with declarations of continued use under Section 8. A mark registered on a foreign basis and never actually used in the United States is a decaying asset, open to cancellation for nonuse and destined to fall at its first maintenance filing.

Forgetting that a Madrid basis is fixed. A Section 66(a) designation cannot be converted to Section 1 or Section 44 if trouble arrives. And trouble arrives as a provisional refusal transmitted through WIPO, with the same strict response clock and the same requirement of U.S. counsel under 37 C.F.R. 2.11. Answering those refusals for foreign holders and their home-country attorneys is the entire design of our Madrid Protocol refusal work.

If a filing is ahead and the basis is not obvious from where you sit, settle it before anything is signed, because it is the one part of an application that is hardest to repair later. Write to the firm with the mark and where it stands, launched or not yet, and you will get a plain answer about which basis fits and what the road to registration looks like from there.
