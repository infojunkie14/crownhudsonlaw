The USPTO Office Action, Explained.

The letter that stops most applicants is, read correctly, a map of exactly what stands between a mark and the register.

By Nicholas K. Harris, Esq. · September 2026

What an Office Action actually is

An Office Action is a formal letter from the examining attorney assigned to a federal trademark application. It states that the application cannot move to registration as filed, sets out every legal ground and every defect the examiner has found, and invites a response. It is not a lawsuit, not a sanction, and not a final decision. It is an ordinary stage of examination, and a large share of applications, including carefully prepared ones, receive at least one.

The examining attorney is a lawyer employed by the USPTO. After an application is filed, it waits in a queue until the examiner reviews the mark against the federal register, the Trademark Act, the Office's own rules, and the application record itself. Anything that blocks registration, and anything in the record that needs correction, goes into the letter. The application then stands still until a complete response arrives or the time to respond runs out.

Everything in the letter falls into one of two categories, and telling them apart is the first skill in reading one.

Substantive refusals versus procedural requirements

A substantive refusal says that the mark itself, as applied to the goods or services in the application, cannot register under the statute. The common grounds:

  • Section 2(d), likelihood of confusion. The examiner cites one or more existing registrations, or earlier-filed applications, and concludes that consumers would likely believe the goods or services come from the same source. This is the most frequent serious refusal and the one most worth an attorney's full attention.
  • Section 2(e)(1), mere descriptiveness. The mark immediately describes a feature, quality, purpose, or characteristic of the goods, and the law will not let one seller take a descriptive term away from competitors who need it.
  • The other Section 2(e) grounds. Marks that are primarily geographically descriptive, deceptively misdescriptive, or primarily merely a surname.
  • Failure to function and ornamentation. The wording appears as decoration or as an informational slogan rather than as an identifier of source.
  • Specimen refusals. In use-based applications, the proof of use does not show the mark actually functioning as a trademark for the listed goods or services.

A procedural requirement, by contrast, asks the applicant to fix the record: an identification of goods and services amended into definite language, correct classification, a disclaimer of an unregistrable component, a translation of foreign wording, a clarified description of the mark, or confirmation of the applicant's entity type and citizenship. One requirement carries special weight. Under 37 C.F.R. 2.11, an applicant domiciled outside the United States must be represented by a U.S.-licensed attorney, and for many foreign applicants the order to appoint counsel arrives in this very letter.

The distinction matters because the two categories call for different work. Requirements are almost always satisfiable, and satisfying them cleanly is largely craft. Refusals present a genuine question of law, and answering them well is the core of a USPTO office action response.

How to read the letter

Office Actions follow a pattern: a summary of the issues near the top, the refusals argued at length with attached evidence, then the requirements, then boilerplate about how and where to respond. Five habits make the reading accurate.

Read every issue, not just the loudest one. A response must address everything the examiner raised. Answering a Section 2(d) refusal brilliantly while ignoring an identification requirement produces an incomplete response, and an incomplete response does not save the application.

Check the scope. Refusals are sometimes partial, applying only to certain classes or certain goods. An application refused in one class may be free to proceed in the others, and knowing that changes the strategy and often the price of the fight.

Study the attachments. The examiner's evidence, cited registrations, dictionary definitions, internet excerpts, is where the argument will be won or lost. A cited registration deserves to be read as carefully as the refusal itself: its exact goods, its channels of trade, the actual wording of the mark.

Note whether the action is nonfinal or final. A first refusal is ordinarily nonfinal, and the applicant holds the full range of responses. A final refusal narrows the road: the realistic paths become a request for reconsideration, an appeal to the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board, or letting the application go.

Watch for advisories. Examiners flag earlier-filed pending applications that may block registration later, and warn when a refusal will be made final if the response does not overcome it. An advisory is not a refusal yet. It is the examiner telling you what happens next.

Why the deadline is unforgiving

The time to respond is fixed by statute and rule, and it is strict and short. There is no negotiating it with the examiner, and the USPTO does not chase applicants who go quiet. When the period ends without a complete response, the application goes abandoned, and the filing fees and the priority date behind it are lost. A petition to revive exists for delays that were unintentional, with its own requirements, its own fee, and its own strict window, and it is a safety net with real holes rather than a plan.

The practical rules are plain. Docket the deadline the day the letter arrives. Treat the true deadline as well before the formal one, because a persuasive response takes time to build: evidence has to be gathered, identifications redrafted, sometimes a declaration prepared and signed. And respond once, completely, rather than in fragments, because a piecemeal answer invites a final refusal.

Argue, amend, or walk away

Every office action response begins with triage, and triage has exactly three honest outcomes.

Argue when the examiner is wrong or the record can be shown in a better light. Likelihood of confusion refusals are often overcome with evidence: real differences between the goods, distinct trade channels, the weakness of the cited mark demonstrated through third-party registrations, differences in sound, appearance, and commercial impression. Examining attorneys do change their minds, and they do it when a response gives them a reasoned, record-supported basis to allow the mark, written for this examiner and this file rather than adapted from a template.

Amend when a change to the application dissolves the problem. A narrower identification of goods can remove the overlap a Section 2(d) refusal rests on. A disclaimer can answer a descriptiveness objection aimed at only part of a mark. In some descriptiveness cases, amending to the Supplemental Register is a sensible harbor while the mark builds distinctiveness through use. Amendments carry consequences, chiefly a narrower registration at the end, so they are decisions to make with open eyes, not concessions to make reflexively.

Walk away when the fight is not worth its cost. Sometimes the cited registration is squarely on point, the descriptiveness is real, and the wiser spend is a stronger mark and a fresh, well-built application through a new U.S. trademark filing. If the application came through the Madrid Protocol

A U.S. designation under Section 66(a) is examined by the same examining attorneys under the same law, and the Office Action travels to the holder as a provisional refusal transmitted through WIPO. The letter tends to arrive in an unfamiliar format, after a delay, with the same strict clock already running, and answering it requires U.S. counsel under 37 C.F.R. 2.11. Foreign holders and their home-country attorneys solve this every day by appointing a U.S. attorney for the single task of answering the refusal, and that arrangement is the whole design of our Madrid Protocol refusal work.
