Amazon Brand Registry and your U.S. trademark.

Amazon built the gate; the USPTO cuts the key. What the Registry asks of a trademark is narrower, and slower to earn, than most sellers expect.

By Nicholas K. Harris, Esq. · September 2026

Contents

  2. Why sellers file with the USPTO at all
  3. The counsel requirement for foreign sellers
  4. The timeline, honestly

Amazon Brand Registry is the reason a meaningful share of new U.S. trademark applications exist at all. Sellers rarely wake up wanting a federal registration for its own sake. They want what Amazon keeps behind the Registry gate: control over their own listings, A+ content, brand analytics, and a complaint channel for counterfeiters and listing hijackers that gets read.

The key to that gate is a trademark. This article covers the Amazon Brand Registry trademark requirements as they operate in practice, why the USPTO filing matters well beyond Amazon, the rule that requires foreign sellers to hire U.S. counsel, and the shortcut that has cost thousands of sellers their applications.

What Brand Registry requires from a trademark

Amazon does not examine trademarks. It defers to government trademark offices, and for the United States marketplace that office is the USPTO. To enroll a brand you need a trademark on the records of an office Amazon accepts, tied to a registration or serial number you can point to. As of this writing, Amazon accepts both issued U.S. registrations and pending USPTO applications, though a pending application is only a provisional key, for reasons covered below.

The requirements that decide real enrollments:

  • The mark must be text, or contain text. Amazon accepts word marks and design marks that include words, letters, or numbers. A pure logo with no readable text does not qualify for enrollment.
  • The text must match the brand name exactly. The name on the trademark record and the brand name you enroll have to line up character for character. ACME GOODS registered and "Acme Goods Co." enrolled is a rejection.
  • The mark must be live. A registration that lapsed for missed maintenance filings, or an application that went abandoned, opens nothing.
  • The mark must appear on your products or packaging, permanently. Amazon asks for images showing the brand name printed, engraved, or otherwise applied during production. A sticker added afterward, or a name that exists only in listing copy, is a common reason enrollments stall.

One mechanic deserves more attention than it gets. Amazon verifies ownership by sending a code to a public contact on the trademark office record, typically the attorney of record or correspondent. If your application was filed by an outfit you can no longer reach, or the email address on your USPTO record belongs to a stranger, that code goes somewhere you cannot follow. Who sits on your record matters operationally, not just legally.

Why sellers file with the USPTO at all

The federal registration is the asset that outlives the policy page.

Amazon could rewrite its enrollment rules next quarter. The federal registration is the asset that outlives the policy page.

A U.S. registration on the Principal Register gives you nationwide priority as of your filing date, a legal presumption that the mark is valid and that you own it, the right to use the ® symbol, and standing to pursue counterfeiters in federal court with statutory remedies behind you. It can be recorded with U.S. Customs and Border Protection to stop infringing imports at the port. None of that depends on Amazon's continued goodwill, and all of it still works against the copycat who moves to another marketplace once you chase him off this one.

Early filing has its own return. A federal application is a public claim that dates from the day it is filed, so every month of delay is a month someone else can file first. In a Section 2(d) dispute over confusingly similar marks, the earlier filing date usually holds the high ground. Our U.S. trademark registration work is built around securing that date with an application that can survive examination, which is a different thing from an application that merely gets submitted.

The counsel requirement for foreign sellers

Since August 2019, every trademark applicant, registrant, or party to a USPTO proceeding whose domicile is outside the United States must be represented by a licensed U.S. attorney. The rule is 37 C.F.R. 2.11, and it has no marketplace exception. A seller domiciled in Shenzhen, London, or Toronto cannot file a U.S. application, respond to a refusal, or maintain a registration without U.S. counsel of record.

This lands squarely on Amazon sellers because so many are domiciled abroad. It also lands on Madrid Protocol filers: a foreign registration extended to the United States under Section 66(a) is examined by the USPTO like any domestic application, and when the USPTO refuses it, the answer must come through U.S. counsel. We treat those as their own category of work; see Madrid Protocol refusals.

The counsel rule is also where the trouble in this industry begins. A regulation that requires an enormous population of foreign applicants to hire a U.S. attorney created a market for attorneys in name only, and the USPTO's reaction to that market is described below.

The timeline, honestly

Plan on the process taking longer than any launch calendar wants it to. A new application waits in the USPTO's queue for months before an examining attorney first reviews it. A large share of applications then receive an Office Action: a refusal or a set of requirements that must be answered on strict, short deadlines, with abandonment as the price of missing them. Refusals under Section 2(d) for likelihood of confusion and under Section 2(e) for descriptiveness are the common ones. After examination comes publication, an opposition window, and only then a certificate. Well over a year from filing to registration is ordinary.

A pending application can open Brand Registry sooner, and that is genuinely useful. It is also fragile. If the application is refused and goes abandoned, the enrollment built on it goes down with it, and the seller is back outside the gate with less than nothing: a public record of a failed filing. That is why the application deserves care before it is filed, a real clearance search and precisely drafted goods and services, and why a refusal deserves a considered answer rather than a shrug. Our Office Action response practice exists because so many applications die at exactly that step.

The filing-mill shortcut, and what it costs

Then there is the cheap route: high-volume filing operations, many of them marketing directly to marketplace sellers, promising a trademark application at a price no reviewing attorney could work for. The mechanics behind that price are now documented at length in USPTO sanctions orders. Attorneys lent their names and signatures to thousands of applications they never read. Specimens were fabricated with photo editing. False U.S. addresses were used to dodge the counsel requirement of 37 C.F.R. 2.11. Applications went in under hijacked or fictitious attorney credentials.

The USPTO's response has been blunt. It has issued sanctions and show-cause orders against these operations, excluded practitioners from federal trademark practice, and terminated tens of thousands of affected applications outright. Terminated means gone: the filing date is lost, the government fees are not refunded, and any Brand Registry enrollment resting on the application collapses with it. The seller starts over, months behind, in line after whoever filed in the meantime.

The signature rules are why none of this is a gray area. Every USPTO filing carries certifications, under 37 C.F.R. 11.18, that the person signing has read it and believes it is legally and factually supported. An application signed by an attorney who never saw it is defective at the root, and the USPTO treats it that way. So when you evaluate any filing service, one question does most of the work: will a named, licensed attorney read my application before signing and filing it? If the answer is muddy, the price is not the bargain it appears to be.

Where this leaves a seller

The sequence that works is unglamorous. Search before you file. File through counsel who actually reads the application, in your true name and domicile. Enroll in Brand Registry on the pending application if Amazon's current policy allows it, treat the registration certificate as the real milestone, and calendar the maintenance filings so the asset stays alive. Sellers who follow that order usually spend less over the life of the brand than sellers who bought the shortcut twice.

If you are planning a Brand Registry enrollment, holding a refusal, or untangling an application someone else filed, we are glad to look at it. Write to the firm with your brand name and where you sell; a short note is enough to start.
