Specimen Refusals: When Your Proof of Use Fails

The Office Action most applicants never see coming is the one that agrees your mark is fine and your evidence is not.

By Nicholas K. Harris, Esq. · September 2026

What a specimen is

Every United States trademark registration rests on a claim of use. The drawing in your application shows the mark you want to protect. The specimen proves that the mark is out in the world doing its job. It is a real example of how customers actually encounter the mark in commerce: the label on the bottle, the tag sewn into the collar, the checkout screen where someone orders your software.

An application based on actual use under Section 1(a) must include a specimen for each class of goods or services. An intent-to-use application under Section 1(b) postpones the question, but only postpones it: no registration issues until a specimen is filed and accepted with the Statement of Use. The requirement never fully goes away, either. Registered owners submit specimens again at maintenance, and the USPTO now audits those filings and strikes goods the owner cannot prove.

The specimen is where more applications stumble than almost anywhere else. A trademark specimen refusal does not mean the examining attorney dislikes your mark. It means your evidence failed, and evidence problems usually have evidence solutions.

What examiners accept: goods versus services

The rules split along the line between trademarks, which cover goods, and service marks, which cover services. Most refusals trace back to an applicant ignoring that line.

For goods, the specimen must show the mark on the goods themselves, on packaging, on labels or tags, or on a display associated with the goods at the point of sale. A webpage qualifies only when it functions as a point of sale: the mark must appear near a picture or description of the goods, together with genuine ordering capability, a price and an add-to-cart button or its equivalent. Under 37 C.F.R. 2.56, a webpage specimen must also show its URL and the date it was accessed or printed. Pure advertising does not work for goods. A press release, a social post promoting the product, an invoice, a trade show flyer: none of these prove use on goods, no matter how polished they look.

For services, the standard is more forgiving. Advertising and marketing materials are acceptable, because a service has no physical surface to carry a label. A website describing the services, a brochure, storefront signage, a vehicle wrap: all of these can serve, provided the specimen shows a direct association between the mark and the specific services named in the application. That last requirement is where service specimens fail. A page that displays the mark in a corner and never says what the company does has proven a name, not a service mark.

The e-commerce mistakes behind most refusals

Online sellers generate more specimen refusals than any other group, and a short list of mistakes accounts for most of them:

  • The mark never appears on the product or its packaging. An Amazon or Etsy listing where the brand name lives only in the listing title or the seller field is not use on the goods. The examiner looks at the photographs, and the photographs show an unbranded product.
  • A marketing page instead of a point of sale. The screenshot shows the mark and the product beautifully, and no way to buy anything. Without ordering information, a webpage is advertising, and advertising fails for goods.
  • The mark appears only in the URL or the store name. Use as a web address or a trade name is not use as a trademark for the goods listed in the application.
  • Missing URL and access date. An otherwise acceptable webpage specimen fails on this formality alone.
  • The specimen shows a different mark than the drawing. You applied for a word mark and the specimen shows a stylized logo with extra wording, or the mark split across elements that read as separate things. The specimen must match what you asked to register.
  • Ornamental use. A slogan printed large across the chest of a T-shirt reads as decoration, not as a brand. The same words on the neck label or the hang tag read as a trademark. Placement decides these cases.

None of this reflects the strength of the underlying brand. These are execution failures, and nearly all of them are avoidable with a careful review of the actual listing before anything is filed. Choosing the right specimen at the start is a core part of a properly prepared U.S. trademark application.

Mockups and altered images: a different kind of problem

Most specimen problems are honest mistakes. This one is not treated like the others. A specimen must show the mark in actual use in commerce, and 37 C.F.R. 2.56 expressly bars mockups, printer's proofs, and digitally created or altered images. A rendering of a product that has never been manufactured, a stock photo with a logo pasted onto it, template artwork from a print-on-demand tool: these are not weak specimens. In the USPTO's eyes they are fabrications.

The Office has spent recent years cracking down hard on fabricated proof of use, terminating tens of thousands of applications connected to doctored specimens and sanctioning the filing operations behind them. Examiners are trained to spot the signs: identical staging across unrelated brands, floating labels, lighting that does not match the surface, packaging with no manufacturing artifacts. The consequences run past a refusal. A use-based application with no genuine use behind it is void, a registration built on one is vulnerable for its entire life, and the specimen was submitted under a signed declaration.

The rule for honest applicants is simple. If the product exists and carries the mark, photograph the real thing. If the product does not exist yet, file under Section 1(b) and say so. An intent-to-use filing is not second class: it preserves your filing date while you get the goods to market, and the specimen comes later, when it can be real.

How a specimen refusal gets fixed

A trademark specimen refusal arrives in an Office Action with a strict, short response deadline, and it is one of the more fixable refusals in trademark practice. The right path depends on the facts.

Submit a substitute specimen. Most refusals resolve this way. The applicant had acceptable evidence all along, the actual packaging rather than the banner ad, and submits it with a sworn statement that it was in use as of the relevant date.

Recapture the evidence. Some refusals are cured by nothing more than a competent screenshot of the same page, retaken so it shows the ordering function, the URL, and the date.

Argue. Examiners are careful, not infallible. Whether a display is associated with the goods, or whether a service specimen shows direct association, is a judgment call, and judgment calls can be argued with evidence and authority.

Amend the basis. If there is no qualifying use yet, the application can usually be amended from Section 1(a) to intent to use under Section 1(b), keeping the original filing date and moving the proof of use to a later stage.

For foreign applicants, a different door. Applications under Section 44(e) and Madrid Protocol extensions under Section 66(a) can register without a U.S. use specimen; the proof-of-use question waits until maintenance. Foreign applicants whose extensions draw refusals are the focus of our Madrid Protocol refusal practice.

What a specimen refusal is not: a reason to abandon a sound application, or to refile from scratch and surrender your original filing date. Weighing which of these routes fits, and whether the refusal is worth answering at all, is exactly the work of an Office Action response.
