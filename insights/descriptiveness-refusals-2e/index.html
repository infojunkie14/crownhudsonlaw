Merely Descriptive? The Section 2(e) Refusal Family

One of the most common refusals in American trademark practice is also the one whose exits are most clearly mapped.

By Nicholas K. Harris, Esq. · September 2026

The refusal most applicants meet first

A merely descriptive trademark refusal is the examining attorney's way of saying that your mark describes the goods instead of identifying their source. It issues under Section 2(e)(1) of the Trademark Act, and it is among the most common substantive refusals the USPTO sends, for a simple reason: business owners name products the way customers talk about them. SPEEDY for a courier service, CRUNCHY for granola, RELIABLE ROOFING for a roofer. That instinct makes for clear marketing and a weak trademark, and the examining attorney is required to say so.

The letter is not a verdict on the business. It is a statement about where the mark sits on a spectrum the law has applied for decades, and the mark's position on that spectrum determines everything that follows: whether the refusal can be argued away, whether the answer is evidence, and whether the Supplemental Register is the right interim home.

The spectrum of distinctiveness

Nearly every descriptiveness fight happens at one border: descriptive versus suggestive.

U.S. trademark law sorts every word mark into five bands, running from unprotectable to strongest.

  • Generic terms name the product itself: BICYCLE for bicycles. A generic term can never serve as a trademark for those goods, and no volume of sales or advertising changes that.
  • Descriptive marks immediately convey a quality, ingredient, feature, function, or characteristic of the goods: CREAMY for yogurt, and laudatory boasts like PREMIUM or THE ULTIMATE, which the law treats as description too. These register on the Principal Register only with proof of acquired distinctiveness.
  • Suggestive marks hint instead of describing, and they demand a mental leap: COPPERTONE for sun care, ROACH MOTEL for insect traps. Suggestive marks register without any additional showing.
  • Arbitrary marks are ordinary words with no connection to the goods: APPLE for computers, SHELL for fuel.
  • Fanciful marks are invented outright: KODAK, XEROX, EXXON. These are the strongest marks the system recognizes.

Nearly every descriptiveness fight happens at one border: descriptive versus suggestive. The traditional test asks whether a consumer needs imagination, thought, or perception to connect the mark with the goods, or whether the mark delivers the information immediately. Reasonable minds differ at that line constantly, which is precisely why these refusals are so often worth answering.

One principle trips up more applicants than any other: descriptiveness is judged in relation to the actual goods and services in the application, never in the abstract. APPLE is arbitrary for computers and generic for fruit. The same word can be a powerful mark in one field and unregistrable in another, which is also why the identification of goods deserves real care at the filing stage, before an examiner ever reads it.

The rest of the Section 2(e) family

Merely descriptive is only the most familiar member of the household. Section 2(e) holds a family of related refusals, and the right response differs for each.

Deceptively misdescriptive, Section 2(e)(1). The mirror image of descriptiveness: the mark describes a quality the goods do not actually have, and consumers would plausibly believe the misdescription. SILK for an all-polyester blouse is the classic example. If the false quality would sway the decision to buy, the refusal escalates to deceptiveness under Section 2(a), an absolute bar with no cure. Where a mark lands between those two provisions is frequently the entire case.

Primarily geographically descriptive, Section 2(e)(2). The mark's primary significance is a place, the goods genuinely come from that place, and consumers would connect the two: BROOKLYN for a Brooklyn brewery. The geography is honest but not distinctive, so the mark follows the same acquired distinctiveness path as any other descriptive term.

Primarily geographically deceptively misdescriptive, Section 2(e)(3). Here the place name is false. The goods do not come from the named place, consumers would believe they do, and that belief would matter to the purchase. For marks adopted after the statute was amended in the 1990s, this refusal cannot be overcome by acquired distinctiveness, which makes it one of the most serious letters in the family.

Primarily merely a surname, Section 2(e)(4). HARRIS, SULLIVAN, GARCIA. The examining attorney weighs how rare the surname is, whether someone connected with the applicant actually bears it, whether the term carries any other recognized meaning, and whether it has the plain look and feel of a surname. Surname refusals run on the descriptiveness playbook: argue the factors, prove acquired distinctiveness, or take the Supplemental Register while distinctiveness accrues. Some of the most famous marks in commerce started here. FORD, DELL, and MCDONALD'S were all somebody's last name first.

A fifth member, functionality under Section 2(e)(5), polices product shapes and features rather than words, and it is a different conversation entirely.

Answering a merely descriptive refusal

There are three honest doors out of a Section 2(e)(1) refusal, and choosing the right one is most of the work.

Argue. Contend the mark is suggestive, not descriptive. Double meanings, incongruity, and wordplay all help: a mark that makes the consumer pause, smile, or think twice is doing something more than describing. SUGAR & SPICE for bakery goods survived on exactly that logic, because the phrase calls up the nursery rhyme before it says anything about ingredients. Ambiguity favors the applicant here, since doubt on the descriptive versus suggestive question is resolved in favor of publishing the mark.

Prove. Concede that the mark is descriptive and show that it has acquired distinctiveness, discussed below.

Reposition. Amend to the Supplemental Register, keep using the mark in commerce, and return for the Principal Register when the evidence exists.

Whichever door fits, the choice must be made against strict, short deadlines: an application that misses its response date goes abandoned. Reading the refusal correctly and giving a plain answer to the question that matters most, whether the fight is worth having at all, is the entire point of our Office Action response practice.

Acquired distinctiveness, in plain terms

Section 2(f) of the Act recognizes what everyone knows from experience: a descriptive term can come to mean one company. AMERICAN AIRLINES describes an American airline, and it also unmistakably names a single carrier. When consumers hear a descriptive term and think of one source, lawyers call it secondary meaning, and the statute calls it acquired distinctiveness.

Proof takes familiar forms: the length and exclusivity of use (the statute lets the USPTO accept five years of substantially exclusive and continuous use as prima facie evidence), sales and advertising figures, unsolicited press coverage, and declarations from customers or the trade. The more descriptive the term, the heavier the showing must be. A mark one shade past suggestive may clear on the use claim alone; a highly descriptive phrase may need a full evidentiary record.

The Supplemental Register, in plain terms

The Supplemental Register is the USPTO's second register, reserved for marks that are capable of distinguishing goods but have not done so yet. It is not a consolation prize, and it is not a trap. It is a parking place with real benefits:

  • The registration appears in USPTO searches, where it warns off later filers and their attorneys.
  • Examining attorneys cite it against later confusingly similar applications under Section 2(d).
  • The owner may use the federal registration symbol.
  • It can serve as a basis for filing in other countries under the Paris Convention.

What it lacks are the presumptions: no legal presumption of validity, ownership, or exclusive rights, and no route to incontestability. The customary path is to hold the Supplemental registration while use continues, then file a new application for the Principal Register under Section 2(f) once distinctiveness has accrued.

One trap deserves a plain warning. An application that reached the USPTO through the Madrid Protocol under Section 66(a) cannot amend to the Supplemental Register at all. Foreign applicants facing a descriptiveness refusal on a Madrid extension hold a narrower set of options, which is exactly why Madrid Protocol refusals are their own discipline in this office.

What this means before you file

The least expensive moment to solve a descriptiveness problem is before it exists. A name one step to the suggestive side of the line registers faster, costs less to prosecute, and is easier to enforce for its entire life. And when the descriptive name is already chosen and already on the products, none of this is fatal: the Section 2(e) family is the most thoroughly charted territory in trademark prosecution, and most of its refusals have marked exits.

If you are holding a refusal now, or weighing a name that sits close to the line, write to the firm and ask. A short question now is cheaper than a long argument later.
