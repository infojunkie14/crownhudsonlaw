Likelihood of confusion: how Section 2(d) refusals work.

Two marks, one marketplace, and an examiner asking a single question: would buyers believe these come from the same place?

By Nicholas K. Harris, Esq. · September 2026

The refusal, in one sentence

A Section 2(d) likelihood of confusion refusal means a USPTO examining attorney searched the federal register, found a registered mark (or an earlier filed application) considered too close to yours, and refused registration under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. 1052(d). The statutory question is whether your mark, used with your goods or services, so resembles the cited mark that ordinary buyers are likely to be confused, mistaken, or deceived about who stands behind the product.

Two features of that standard shape everything else. First, the test is likelihood, not bare possibility. Some conceivable overlap is not enough; the examining attorney must build a record showing confusion is probable, and the refusal can be attacked wherever that record is thin. Second, the refusal is one attorney's legal position stated in a letter, and it invites a written answer. It is not a verdict. Applications survive 2(d) refusals every week, on argument and evidence, inside a strict, short response window.

The DuPont factors, in plain language

The framework comes from In re E. I. du Pont de Nemours & Co., a 1973 decision listing thirteen factors that courts, the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board, and examining attorneys weigh. Nobody weighs all thirteen in any one case. In examination, the analysis almost always turns on a short list:

  • Similarity of the marks in appearance, sound, meaning, and overall commercial impression.
  • Relatedness of the goods or services: would consumers expect them to come from a single source?
  • Trade channels and buyers: do the products reach the same customers in the same way?
  • Purchase conditions: impulse purchases are confused easily; expensive, carefully researched purchases far less so.
  • Strength of the cited mark, including how crowded the register and the marketplace are with similar marks.
  • Consent: whether the owner of the cited mark has agreed in writing that the two can coexist.

Most refusals stand or fall on the first two. When the marks are close and the goods are plainly related, the remaining factors rarely rescue an application. When either of the first two genuinely favors the applicant, the refusal is vulnerable.

Similarity of the marks

Marks are compared as wholes, on sight, sound, connotation, and the total impression they leave in a buyer's memory. But "as wholes" does not mean every element counts equally. Dominant elements carry more weight: descriptive or disclaimed wording counts for little, the literal wording of a mark usually outweighs its design features, and the first word of a phrase tends to do most of the identifying work.

Similarity in any single dimension can be enough. Marks spelled differently but pronounced alike are treated as highly similar, because word of mouth and voice search do not care about spelling. Marks that neither look nor sound alike can still be refused for meaning the same thing, which is how a foreign word and its English translation can collide under the doctrine of foreign equivalents.

The practical consequence: a response that lists surface differences ("ours has a hyphen and a logo") persuades nobody. What moves an examining attorney is a difference in the part of the mark that consumers would actually use to identify the source, or a real difference in what the two marks mean.

Relatedness of the goods and services

The goods do not have to be identical, or even competitive. Wine and restaurant services have been found related; so have clothing and retail store services featuring clothing. The question is whether consumers, seeing similar marks on the two sets of goods, would assume a common source. Examining attorneys prove relatedness with evidence: third-party registrations covering both kinds of goods under one mark, and web pages showing the same companies selling both. A response can attack that evidence, and should, when it is generic or stale.

One rule surprises almost everyone: the comparison runs on the identifications as written, not on the real marketplace. An application for "clothing" with no limits is deemed to cover every kind of clothing, moving through every ordinary trade channel to every ordinary buyer, whatever the applicant actually sells. This is why drafting the identification is genuine legal work, and why a carefully built application, of the kind we prepare in our U.S. trademark registration practice, avoids some 2(d) fights before they start.

Arguments that actually work

Different commercial impression. The strongest similarity arguments reframe the comparison around meaning. If the shared word carries a different connotation in each mark, or sits in a different grammatical role, the overall impressions can diverge even where the letters overlap. This argument is won with dictionaries, usage evidence, and the marks themselves, not with adjectives.

Weakness of the cited mark. When the register is crowded with marks sharing the same element for related goods, that element is weak, and consumers learn to distinguish marks on small differences. A well-documented crowded-field showing, built from live third-party registrations and marketplace uses, narrows the cited mark's scope of protection and can carry the response on its own.

Narrowing the identification. Amending the goods or services to a specific type, channel, or customer class can pull the two marks out of each other's orbit. The amendment must stay within the scope of what was originally filed, so this door only opens as wide as the original drafting allowed.

Sophisticated purchasers. Where both identifications are limited to expensive goods bought deliberately by professionals, care in purchasing weighs against confusion. This argument fails when the identifications, as written, still reach ordinary consumers.

The applicant's own prior registration. An applicant who already owns a registration for substantially the same mark and goods, one that has coexisted with the cited mark, can argue that the new application changes nothing in the marketplace. Examining attorneys take that history seriously.

What rarely works: pointing to a logo or stylization the cited mark lacks, describing marketplace facts the written identifications contradict, or insisting that no confusion has occurred when the marks have barely coexisted. Arguments an examiner has read a thousand times get the answer written a thousand times.

Consent and coexistence, the basics

One DuPont factor belongs to the cited registrant: consent. A consent agreement is the registrant's written statement that it consents to your registration and does not believe confusion is likely. DuPont itself said such agreements deserve great weight, on the sensible ground that the parties closest to the marketplace understand it best, and examining attorneys are instructed not to substitute their own judgment for a credible consent.

Credible is the load-bearing word. A "naked" consent, a bare sentence with no reasons, earns little weight. A detailed consent recites why confusion is unlikely (different goods, channels, customers, or territories) and what each party will do to keep buyers from being confused. A coexistence agreement goes further still: it is a contract governing both sides' use going forward, and it can be worth negotiating even when a consent alone would satisfy the USPTO.

Approaching a registrant is a strategic decision, not a form letter. It reveals your interest in the mark, it opens a negotiation you cannot fully control, and it should be weighed against the strength of the arguments available on the record alone.

When the cited registration is the real problem

Some cited registrations are dead weight: marks no longer in use, held by owners who have moved on. The register has tools for that. Expungement and reexamination proceedings under the Trademark Modernization Act target registrations covering goods on which the mark was never used, or was not in use when the registrant claimed it was; a petition to cancel at the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board reaches further; and a registration whose maintenance filings lapse clears the path on its own. When the citation is an earlier filed application rather than a registration, the refusal is provisional and the application can sit suspended while the earlier one succeeds or fails. Choosing among these paths is a judgment about cost, timing, and how much the mark is worth to the client.

Refusals against Madrid filings

Foreign applicants who designate the United States under Section 66(a) of the Lanham Act face exactly the same Section 2(d) analysis. The refusal arrives as a provisional refusal notified through WIPO, and because 37 C.F.R. 2.11 requires foreign-domiciled applicants to be represented by U.S.-licensed counsel, answering it means engaging a U.S. attorney. Our Madrid Protocol refusal practice exists for precisely this situation.

Answering one well

A good 2(d) response is built on the record: it takes the examining attorney's evidence apart piece by piece, supplies contrary evidence of its own, and argues the factors that genuinely favor the applicant instead of reciting all thirteen. It also starts with an honest triage, because some refusals are better answered by amendment than argument, and a few are not worth answering at all. That triage, and the response itself, is the core of our Office Action response work.
