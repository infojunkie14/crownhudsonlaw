Trademark Scam Letters: Spotting Fake USPTO Notices.

The most official-looking mail a trademark owner receives is very often the mail the government never sent.

By Nicholas K. Harris, Esq. · September 2026

Contents

  2. The common species
  3. How genuine USPTO correspondence behaves
  4. The WIPO-adjacent fake registries

Why the letters find you

File a federal trademark application and, within weeks, the mail starts. Little of it comes from the USPTO. Most of it comes from an industry that exists because the trademark system is public and most applicants are new to it.

Every application becomes a public record the moment it is filed. The owner's name and mailing address, the mark, the serial number, the filing date, the goods and services: all of it is published in the USPTO's open databases, and later in registration records and international gazettes. That openness is a feature of the system, since the register works by giving the world notice of who claims what. It also hands a ready-made mailing list to anyone willing to mine it, and an entire industry does exactly that.

The solicitations these operations send are engineered to be paid, not read. They carry eagles, seals and serif type, Washington or Alexandria return addresses that are often mail drops, and names assembled from the words United States, Trademark, Patent, Register, Office and Agency in varying order. They quote your real serial number and your real mark, because those are public. And they arrive timed to real events in the life of an application: filing, publication, registration, the statutory maintenance windows. The timing is the trick. A trademark owner who knows a deadline exists somewhere, but not what the government's mail looks like, is the ideal recipient of a fake USPTO letter, and the trademark scam economy is tuned to reach that person in exactly that week.

Both the USPTO and WIPO publish standing warnings about this mail, and the USPTO posts examples of it along with the names it was sent under. The examples keep accumulating, because an operation shut down under one name reappears under another. The defense is not knowing every name. It is knowing how the genuine article behaves.

The common species

The scam economy works on owners who cannot name their own baseline: who represents them, what is due next, what the government's mail looks like.

The mail sorts into a handful of recurring shapes.

  • The lookalike invoice. A document formatted as a bill: an amount due, a pay-by date, a remittance slip, your mark and serial number printed accurately. The fine print, where it exists at all, concedes that the document is a solicitation and that the sender is not affiliated with any government agency. The design assumes the fine print will not be read, and the disclaimers are usually the only true sentences on the page.
  • The private register. An offer to publish, record or catalog your mark in an international directory. No examiner consults these directories, no court gives them weight, and no customs officer has heard of them. A listing there has no legal effect anywhere in the world. The payment buys a line in a private database and nothing else.
  • The premature renewal service. Registrations do carry genuine maintenance obligations, under Sections 8 and 9 of the Trademark Act for domestic registrations and Section 71 for extensions of protection, and those filings come due on a fixed statutory schedule. The solicitations arrive far ahead of that schedule, quote heavily marked-up charges, and count on the recipient not knowing where the schedule actually stands.
  • The impersonation contact. A call or email claiming to be the USPTO itself: there is a problem with your application, it faces abandonment or cancellation, pay now or confirm your personal details. Caller ID can be spoofed and email display names forged. The USPTO does not call, email or text applicants to ask for payment or personal information, and no genuine government fee is ever collected by gift card or a wire to an individual.
  • The bait-price agency. Not a letter but an advertisement: registration at a price too low to cover the government's own filing fee, offered by an entity that is not a law firm and sometimes borrowing the name and credentials of a real attorney who never saw the file. The USPTO has terminated tens of thousands of applications tainted by false attorney signatures and fabricated specimens. The cheap filing is not merely weak work; it can poison the application itself.

How genuine USPTO correspondence behaves

Telling the real thing from the imitation takes one habit and a few fixed facts.

The habit: verify against the file. Every genuine document in a federal trademark application appears in TSDR, the USPTO's public status and document system, under the serial or registration number. Office Actions, notices, certificates, every letter the Office actually issued. If a document claiming to be official is not in the file, the question is answered. This one free check disposes of nearly every fake in circulation.

Official email comes from uspto.gov. The exact domain, not a variant with an extra word, a hyphen, or a different ending. A sender address is easy to skim past and easy to forge in the display name, so read the part after the @ sign, and then verify in TSDR anyway.

Correspondence goes to the attorney of record. When an application names counsel, the USPTO directs its correspondence there. A payment demand landing directly in a represented owner's mailbox deserves to be treated as a solicitation until proven otherwise.

Government fees are paid to the USPTO, inside its own systems. The Office does not send invoices for registration through the post, and no genuine fee is ever payable to a private company on a letter's say-so.

Real notices instruct; they do not sell. Genuine correspondence cites the statute or rule it rests on, states what is required, and explains how to respond. It offers no premium tier and no expedited upgrade payable to the sender. A real refusal is answered on the law, and that answer is the work described in our Office Action response service.

The WIPO-adjacent fake registries

Owners who extend protection abroad through the Madrid Protocol meet a second wave. An international registration is published in WIPO's gazette, which is another public record and another mined mailing list. The invoices that follow come from entities whose names and initials sit deliberately close to WIPO's, set in institutional typography, quoting sums in Swiss francs or euros for registration, publication or renewal in databases that are private catalogs with no legal standing in any country.

WIPO publishes warnings and reproductions of these invoices, and the underlying pattern is stable because the genuine cast of a Madrid filing is short: the trademark office where the base application lives, WIPO's International Bureau in Geneva, and the national office of each designated country. At no point in the procedure is money owed to anyone else. An invoice from outside that chain can be discarded on that ground alone, before reading a word of it.

One genuine document travels through this channel and gets mistaken for junk in the opposite direction: the provisional refusal. When the USPTO refuses a U.S. designation, the refusal reaches the holder through WIPO, in an unfamiliar format, and answering it requires U.S. counsel under 37 C.F.R. 2.11. Discarding that letter as one more fake can cost far more than any bogus invoice would have, and sorting the two apart is the front end of our Madrid Protocol refusal work.

What to do with a suspicious notice

Do not pay, do not reply, and do not call the number printed on it. Any response confirms a live, attentive recipient, and paid names are resold to the next operation.

Check the record. TSDR for a U.S. application or registration, Madrid Monitor for an international one. If the document is not in the official file, you are holding a solicitation, whatever its letterhead says.

Send it to counsel. An attorney with the matter docketed already knows what is actually due and when, and can dispose of a fake in minutes. That routing is quiet insurance in itself: when a filing is made through counsel, official correspondence flows to the firm and is docketed on arrival, so a demand arriving anywhere else exposes itself immediately. That is how our U.S. trademark registration service is built to run.

Report it. The USPTO asks recipients to forward misleading solicitations and impersonation emails to it, and it publishes examples of the ones it confirms. Deceptive solicitations can also be reported to the Federal Trade Commission, and the international registry invoices to WIPO. If money has already moved, contact the card issuer or bank promptly; recovering it from the sender is rarely realistic.

Do not overcorrect. The quieter damage this industry does is teaching owners that all trademark mail is fake. Then a genuine Office Action or maintenance notice goes unread, the clock runs, and the application or registration is lost to silence. The answer to fraudulent mail is verification, never deafness.

The honest reference point

The scam economy works on owners who cannot name their own baseline: who represents them, what is due next, what the government's mail looks like. Establish the baseline and the imitations become obvious, because every one of them fails a check the real system passes.

The same checks measure any firm you might hire, this one included. A legitimate practice is verifiable in the very records the scammers mine: a named attorney, an attorney-of-record entry on the filings, a bar admission that can be looked up, a street address, and flat fees, stated in writing, before any engagement, as ours are on the pricing page. Honest trademark work never needs to dress as the government, because it sells judgment rather than fear.

If a notice has arrived and you cannot tell which side of the line it sits on, ask. Write to the firm with the serial or registration number and a copy of the letter, and you will get a plain answer: genuine or not, and if genuine, what it actually requires.
