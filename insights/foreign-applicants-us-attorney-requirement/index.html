Why Foreign Trademark Applicants Need a U.S. Attorney

A rule the USPTO adopted in 2019 quietly redrew the map for every applicant who lives outside the United States, and it forgives very little.

By Nicholas K. Harris, Esq. · September 2026

The rule, in one paragraph

Since August 3, 2019, every trademark applicant, registrant, and party to a Trademark Trial and Appeal Board proceeding whose domicile lies outside the United States and its territories must be represented at the USPTO by an attorney licensed in a U.S. state or territory. The requirement is codified at 37 C.F.R. 2.11, and it reaches everything: new applications, maintenance and renewal filings for existing registrations, and oppositions and cancellations before the Board. It applies whatever the filing basis, whether use in commerce under Section 1(a), intent to use under Section 1(b), a home-country registration under Section 44(e), or an international registration extended to the United States under Section 66(a). This is the USPTO attorney requirement for foreign applicants, and an applicant who ignores it does not save money. That applicant simply files an application that cannot proceed.

Who counts as foreign-domiciled

Domicile is the operative word, and the USPTO defines it more narrowly than most applicants expect. For an individual, domicile is the place the person resides and intends to be a principal home. For a company, it is the principal place of business: the headquarters where the officers or senior executives actually direct and control the business. A company organized abroad that sells heavily into the United States is still foreign-domiciled. So is a founder who runs a Delaware LLC from Shenzhen, London, or São Paulo, at least as to any application filed in the founder's own name.

The Office polices the address on record. A post office box, a mail-forwarding service, a registered-agent address, or an address given in care of someone else does not establish U.S. domicile, and examining attorneys are authorized to demand documentary proof when an address looks like a mailbox rather than a home or a headquarters. Listing a borrowed U.S. address to dodge the rule is not a loophole. It is a misrepresentation in a federal filing, and the USPTO has shown every willingness to sanction it.

The counsel side of the rule is broader than many foreign applicants assume. Any attorney who is an active member in good standing of the bar of any U.S. state, commonwealth, territory, or the District of Columbia qualifies. Trademark prosecution is federal practice, so the attorney's state of licensure does not need to match anything about the applicant or the goods. A Canadian attorney or agent may be recognized in a limited additional role for a Canadian client, but a qualified U.S. attorney must still be appointed, and only that attorney can sign responses and drive the file.

Why the USPTO adopted it in 2019

Through the late 2010s the USPTO absorbed an enormous surge of applications from overseas, most of them unrepresented. Too many arrived with doctored specimens, mock-ups presented as real product photos, and claims of use that no one had verified. The federal register is a public resource: every inaccurate registration on it blocks honest applicants during clearance and weakens the value of every legitimate mark. At the same time, foreign filing agencies with no license anywhere in the United States were preparing and prosecuting applications in bulk, which is the unauthorized practice of law before the Office.

The agency's answer was structural. Require a licensed U.S. attorney, answerable to a state bar and to the USPTO's own rules of professional conduct, on every foreign-domiciled file, and the accuracy problem acquires a responsible human with something to lose. The rule also brought the United States into line with much of the world; a large share of trademark offices abroad have long required foreign applicants to act through local counsel, and American applicants filing overseas comply with those rules every day.

The years since have shown the Office means it. The USPTO has issued show-cause and sanctions orders against filing schemes that circumvented the rule, terminated tens of thousands of applications that carried unauthorized or fabricated attorney information, and excluded attorneys who lent their signatures to high-volume mills without genuinely reviewing the work. The requirement is treated as an integrity measure, not a formality.

What happens to an application filed without counsel

An application filed directly by a foreign-domiciled applicant without U.S. counsel is not rejected at the door. It is examined, and the examining attorney issues an Office Action requiring the appointment of a qualified U.S. attorney, alongside whatever substantive refusals the application has earned on its own, a Section 2(d) likelihood of confusion refusal or an identification requirement being the usual companions. The response deadline is strict and short, and an application that misses it goes abandoned.

The cure is appointment, and only appointment. The response itself must come from the newly appointed U.S. attorney; a response signed by the foreign applicant personally does not satisfy the requirement, however complete its arguments. This is where Office Action response work and the counsel requirement usually meet: the appointed attorney answers the counsel requirement and the substantive refusals in a single, properly signed filing.

The worse outcomes belong to applicants who tried to route around the rule. Applications carrying attorney information the named attorney never authorized, or a false U.S. domicile address, have been terminated by sanctions order rather than merely refused, and money already paid to the USPTO stays paid. An abandoned or terminated application also surrenders its filing date, which is often the most valuable thing it had.

Madrid filings: the exception that is not really one

Requests for extension of protection under Section 66(a) reach the USPTO through the International Bureau in Geneva, so there is no U.S. filing moment at which counsel can be demanded up front. If examination turns up no ground for refusal at all, the extension can proceed to registration without a U.S. attorney ever appearing. That is the whole exception, and it is thinner than it sounds, because most applications do not clear examination untouched.

The moment a provisional refusal issues, the counsel requirement arrives with it. The representative recorded at WIPO cannot respond to the USPTO, whatever their standing at home. The refusal must be answered by a U.S.-licensed attorney, appointed for the purpose, within the same strict deadline that governs any other Office Action. Foreign holders and their home-country counsel who plan for this in advance, rather than discovering it inside the response window, keep their options open. How to comply, cleanly

Compliance is not complicated. It is simply earlier and more substantive than most foreign applicants expect.

  • Appoint counsel before filing, not after refusal. An attorney who runs the clearance search, tightens the identification of goods, and vets the specimen before submission prevents the refusals a late-appointed attorney can only argue about. This is what a properly built U.S. trademark registration engagement looks like.
  • Expect real credentials on the record. The filing must carry the attorney's name, office address, and bar information, and the attorney signs. If a service will not tell you who your attorney is, you do not have one.
  • Give your true domicile address. A genuine foreign address plus genuine U.S. counsel satisfies the rule completely. A borrowed U.S. address satisfies nothing and risks everything.
  • Be suspicious of an attorney "included" at an implausible price. A signature rented by a volume agency is exactly the practice the USPTO has been terminating, and the client's application is what absorbs the damage.
  • Keep the relationship alive after registration. Maintenance and renewal filings by a foreign-domiciled registrant need U.S. counsel too, and USPTO correspondence flows through the attorney of record.

For foreign firms and agents

Nothing in 37 C.F.R. 2.11 takes the client away from the trademark attorney or agent who handles the home portfolio. It requires a U.S. attorney for the U.S. leg, and the workable structure is associate counsel: the foreign firm keeps the relationship and the strategy, and U.S. counsel signs, files, and answers the USPTO.
