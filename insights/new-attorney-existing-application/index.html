Changing Attorneys Mid-Application: How Takeover Representation Works.

The application belongs to the applicant, not to whoever filed it, and when representation fails, the file can move on to counsel who will finish the job.

By Nicholas K. Harris, Esq. · September 2026

Why applicants change counsel mid-application

A federal trademark application often outlives the arrangement that produced it. It moves at the USPTO's pace, and in the Office's eyes the person handling it is replaceable at the applicant's word. When people ask a new trademark attorney to take over an application, the reasons come from a short list, and none of them is exotic.

The filing service hit its ceiling. Non-attorney filing companies can get an application on file, but they cannot lawfully represent anyone before the USPTO, and examination is where that limit bites. When an Office Action arrives raising a real refusal, the service has nothing left to sell. Some say so plainly. Others go quiet, and the applicant discovers the limit only when the deadline is close.

The prior attorney was sanctioned or excluded. The USPTO has spent the last several years sanctioning practitioners who lent their names to high-volume filing operations without genuinely reviewing the work, and terminating applications connected to them. When the attorney of record is excluded from practice before the Office, every client file that attorney held needs new counsel, through no fault of the applicants.

The attorney simply stopped answering. No misconduct, no drama, just silence while a deadline approaches. An applicant does not need the prior attorney's cooperation, or even a reply, to move the file.

The applicant self-filed and got refused. Filing without counsel is lawful for U.S.-domiciled applicants and works well enough until examination. A refusal under Section 2(d) or Section 2(e)(1) is a legal argument, not a form to fill in, and this is the moment most self-filers decide the application deserves a professionally built response.

The Office ordered it. Under 37 C.F.R. 2.11, an applicant domiciled outside the United States must be represented by a U.S.-licensed attorney. Foreign applicants who filed alone, or through a service that was never counsel at all, receive a requirement to appoint one, and the appointment is not optional.

How appearance and revocation work in practice

The mechanics are simpler than most applicants fear. The USPTO recognizes a practitioner under 37 C.F.R. 2.17, and once recognized, that attorney is the one the Office corresponds with. Recognition ordinarily continues until the application registers or goes abandoned, or until the applicant revokes the appointment or the attorney properly withdraws.

The switch itself is a filing, not a negotiation. The applicant revokes the prior appointment and appoints new counsel, and in current practice both steps travel together in a single TEAS submission that updates the attorney and correspondence information in the record. The revocation is signed by the applicant personally, or by someone with legal authority to bind a business applicant. The incoming attorney prepares the paperwork; the client signs it.

Three things about this step surprise people reliably.

The prior attorney's permission is not required. No countersignature, no exit interview, no release. The right to choose counsel belongs to the client, and the record simply updates. A fee dispute with the prior firm is a private matter, and it has no power to hold the application hostage.

The file is already public. Every document in the prosecution history is readable in the USPTO's TSDR system by anyone holding the serial number. A prospective new attorney can read the entire record, and give a straight assessment of it, before you sign anything.

Deadlines do not move. The response clock set by an Office Action keeps running straight through the handover. Changing attorneys is not a ground for more time and the Office will not treat it as one, which is why the appointment and the substantive work should be planned together rather than one after the other.

What a new attorney needs from you

A takeover engagement starts with a short set of materials, most of which you already have.

  • The serial number. From it, the whole official record is retrievable: the application as filed, every Office Action, every response, every notice. This one number does most of the work.
  • The story the record does not tell. What the prior attorney or filing service told you, what you paid for, and copies of the emails. The gap between what the record shows and what you were told is often where the real problem lives.
  • Ownership facts. Who owns the mark today. If the business was reorganized, sold, or renamed since filing, say so early, because ownership defects are among the least forgiving problems in trademark practice.
  • Evidence of use. How and where the mark appears in commerce: product photos, packaging, the live website, dates. Specimen problems are common in troubled applications, and fresh, honest evidence is often the cure.
  • Every deadline you know of. The new attorney will docket everything independently from the record, but a letter you received last month is worth mentioning today rather than surfacing later.

Notice what is not on the list: your USPTO.gov account and its password. New counsel appears through the attorney's own account and never needs yours. A representative who asks for your login credentials is describing exactly the practice the Office has been sanctioning.

What can be fixed late, and what cannot

A change of counsel usually happens mid-problem, so the practical question is what a new trademark attorney taking over the application can still repair. The answer is more than applicants fear and less than they hope, and it splits cleanly.

Still fixable, while the relevant window is open. An unanswered Office Action can be answered completely, refusals argued and requirements satisfied, right up to the deadline. An identification of goods and services can be clarified or narrowed. A defective specimen can often be replaced with a substitute supported by a proper declaration. A disclaimer can be entered, a translation supplied, an entity clarified. In some descriptiveness cases, amendment to the Supplemental Register remains on the table. Even an application that has gone abandoned can sometimes be revived by petition, where the failure to respond was unintentional, inside a strict window fixed by rule; that petition is a safety net with real holes in it, not a plan.

Beyond repair. Some defects sit in the foundation. The identification can never be broadened; goods dropped or never claimed cannot be added to this application. The mark itself cannot be materially altered under 37 C.F.R. 2.72; a redesigned logo is a new application, not an amendment. An application filed by a party who did not own the mark is void, and no substitution of counsel cures it. A deadline that has fully lapsed, past the reach of revival, is gone, and the filing date behind it goes with it. And statements already made to the Office that were not true cannot be quietly unmade; what a new attorney can do is assess the damage candidly and chart the cleanest lawful path forward, which is sometimes a corrected record and sometimes a fresh start.

When the fresh start is the right answer, it should be said plainly. A well-built new application with the old defects designed out often costs less, in money and in months, than heroic surgery on a doomed one. A practice that recommends rescue in every case is selling procedures, not judgment.

If your application came through a filing mill

One version of this story deserves its own section, because the applicants caught in it did nothing wrong. In recent years the USPTO has moved against high-volume operations, many based overseas, that filed enormous numbers of applications using attorney names without authority, recycled or fabricated specimens, and credentials that were never real. The Office has terminated proceedings, sanctioned the participants, and scrutinized the applications they touched.

If your application was filed through such an operation, the record may show an attorney you never spoke to, a specimen you never provided, or a correspondence address you do not recognize. The path forward is the ordinary takeover machinery pointed at an extraordinary mess: appoint genuine counsel, review everything filed under your name, correct what the rules allow, answer whatever the Office has demanded, and be candid about the rest. Some of these applications are salvageable and some are not, and the only way to learn which is yours is to read the file.

If your application is stalled and the person who filed it is gone, silent, or barred from helping, the file can still move. Write to the firm with the serial number, and you will get a plain assessment of what the record shows, what remains fixable, and whether taking the application over is worth doing at all.
