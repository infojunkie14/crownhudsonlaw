Your U.S. Trademark Application Was Refused. Now What?

Five paths lead out of a refusal. The expensive mistake is not choosing the wrong one. It is failing to choose at all.

By Nicholas K. Harris, Esq. · September 2026

Contents

  2. The five paths
  3. When fighting is worth it
  4. When it is not
  5. What abandonment actually costs

First, name what refused you

When a United States trademark application is refused, what to do next depends almost entirely on what kind of refusal it is. The refusal arrives inside an Office Action, a formal letter from the examining attorney, and everything that follows, the strategy, the cost, the odds, the timeline, turns on an accurate reading of that letter. Refusals are not one thing, and treating them as one thing is how applicants spend money answering the wrong question.

The first cut is between substantive refusals and procedural requirements. A requirement asks you to fix the record: a sharper identification of goods and services, a disclaimer of an unregistrable word, a translation of foreign wording, a clarified description of the mark. Requirements are almost always satisfiable and rarely decide an application's fate. A substantive refusal says the mark itself, as applied to these goods or services, cannot register under the statute. That is where triage begins.

The common substantive grounds, and what each one implies about your options:

  • Section 2(d), likelihood of confusion. The examiner has cited an existing registration and concluded that consumers would likely mistake the source of the goods. Where the obstacle is an earlier-filed application rather than a registration, the examiner suspends yours instead, until that application registers or dies. This is the most frequent serious refusal. It rests on a comparison of two marks and two sets of goods, and the comparison is answered with evidence.
  • Section 2(e)(1), mere descriptiveness. The mark describes the goods rather than distinguishing them. The realistic answers here include argument, a disclaimer, a claim of acquired distinctiveness, or the Supplemental Register.
  • Surname and geographic refusals under Section 2(e). Close cousins of descriptiveness, each with its own test and its own escape routes.
  • Failure to function, including ornamentation. The wording appears as decoration or as an informational slogan rather than as a source identifier. Sometimes this is a specimen problem in disguise. Sometimes it is a sign that the "mark" is not a mark.
  • Specimen refusals. The proof of use does not show the mark doing trademark work. Often fully fixable with a substitute specimen; occasionally fatal to a use-based filing that was simply premature.

Two more readings complete the triage. Is the action nonfinal or final? A nonfinal action leaves the whole menu open, while a final one narrows the road to a request for reconsideration filed together with a notice of appeal to the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board, an appeal alone, a petition to the Director where the only item maintained is a procedural requirement, or a deliberate exit. A request for reconsideration filed on its own, with no notice of appeal, is an incomplete response, and the application abandons when the response period runs. And is the refusal total or partial? A refusal aimed at one class, or at certain goods within a class, can often be resolved by deleting or dividing out the contested part, so the rest of the application proceeds while you decide whether the remainder is worth a fight.

The five paths

A response drafted to lose slowly, filed so that nobody has to admit the name is dead, buys nothing but delay.

Every refused application has exactly five realistic endings. The work is choosing among them deliberately instead of arriving at one by default.

Argue. Respond on the merits and persuade the examiner to withdraw the refusal. Confusion refusals are argued with the realities of the marketplace: differences in the goods, in trade channels, in purchasers, the weakness of the cited mark shown through third-party registrations, differences in sound, appearance, and commercial impression. Descriptiveness refusals are argued at the line between describing and suggesting, which is genuinely blurry and therefore genuinely arguable. Examining attorneys withdraw refusals every day, and they do it when a response gives them a reasoned, record-supported basis to allow the mark.

Amend. Change the application so the refusal no longer fits it. Narrowing the identification of goods can remove the overlap a Section 2(d) refusal rests on. A disclaimer can neutralize a descriptiveness objection aimed at a single word of a composite mark. Amendment buys certainty at the price of scope: the registration that issues is narrower than the one applied for, and whether that trade is acceptable is a business judgment before it is a legal one.

Take the Supplemental Register. For descriptiveness refusals, amending to the Supplemental Register is often the practical middle path. A supplemental registration is not the full prize; it carries no presumption of validity or exclusive right. But it permits use of the federal registration symbol, it surfaces in the clearance searches others run before adopting names, it can be cited against later confusingly similar applications, and after a period of continued use it supports a fresh Principal Register filing built on acquired distinctiveness. For a young brand with a descriptive name, it is often the practical choice.

Refile. Sometimes the application, not the mark, is the problem: the wrong filing basis, a doomed specimen, an identification drafted so broadly that it invited every objection available. A clean second filing, built around the refusal you now understand, can be cheaper and faster than rehabilitating the first. And sometimes the mark is the problem, in which case refiling really means a stronger mark and a fresh, well-built U.S. application behind it.

Abandon. Let the application go, on purpose, because the fight is not worth its cost. This is a legitimate outcome, and occasionally the smartest one on the table. What it costs is set out below, so that choosing it, or drifting into it, happens with the price tag in view.

When fighting is worth it

A refusal is worth fighting when three things line up: a real weakness in the examiner's position, a mark that matters, and arithmetic that favors the response.

The weakness comes first. A Section 2(d) citation is vulnerable when the cited registration covers different goods moving through different channels to different buyers, when the shared element is weak and diluted across a crowded register, or when the marks part ways in sound and commercial impression once they are compared as wholes rather than as fragments. A descriptiveness refusal is vulnerable when the term takes a mental step to connect to the goods, or when the examiner's own evidence shows the phrase used in many senses rather than one. The examiner's attachments usually reveal, to a careful reader, exactly how much conviction sits behind the refusal.

The mark comes second. A name that is on the product, in the domain, in the advertising, and in customers' memories is worth real effort, because the alternative is a rebrand, and a rebrand nearly always costs more than a response. A name the business adopted last quarter and could shed without a scar deserves a colder calculation.

The arithmetic comes third, and it should be knowable in advance. A response is a single, defined piece of legal work, which is why we handle Office Action responses for flat fees, stated in writing, with the triage delivered before you commit to the fight. If the record says the refusal will likely stand, that conclusion belongs in the first email, not the last one.

When it is not

Do not fight a refusal that is simply correct. When the cited mark is close to identical, the goods overlap squarely, and the trade channels are the same, argument does not change the register; it only postpones the same result at a higher price. When the mark is a term every competitor plainly needs to describe the same goods, the descriptiveness refusal is the statute working as intended.

Do not fight out of momentum. The filing fee already spent is gone whether or not a response follows it, and money already lost is a poor reason to lose more. The question is never what the application cost; it is what the next step buys.

And do not fight to defer a decision. A response drafted to lose slowly, filed so that nobody has to admit the name is dead, buys nothing but delay. When the realistic paths have collapsed to refiling or walking away, the professional answer is to say so plainly and put the remaining budget where it can still do work.

What abandonment actually costs

Abandonment has a precise price, and it is worth itemizing before the response window runs out, because every item on the list is cheaper to keep than to buy back.

The government filing fees are not refunded; they paid for the examination that produced the refusal. The filing date dies with the application, and the filing date is the quiet asset: it is the priority that outranks everyone who filed after you. Abandon and refile later, and every application filed in the gap now stands ahead of yours, including, sometimes, a competitor who watched your application go abandoned and drew the obvious conclusion. The prosecution record is public and permanent, and the refusal that stopped this application will greet the examiner who picks up the next one for the same mark. And the obstacle itself remains: the cited registration does not expire because you stopped responding to it.

There is a difference between choosing abandonment and defaulting into it. The applicant who decides to let a filing go can time a refiling, adjust the mark or the goods, and keep continuous use in the market so a later acquired-distinctiveness claim stays available. The applicant who goes silent gets none of that, plus a scramble: revival after an unintended lapse exists as a petition with its own fee, its own paperwork, and its own strict window, and it is a safety net for accidents, not a strategy. Applications that reached the USPTO through the Madrid Protocol add one more wrinkle: the refusal travels to the holder by way of WIPO, the response period cannot be extended, and the requirement to appoint U.S. counsel under 37 C.F.R. 2.11 has to be met before anything can be filed.

Read the letter, name the refusal, and pick a path while all five are still open. That is the whole discipline.
