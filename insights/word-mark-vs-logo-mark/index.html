Word Mark or Logo? What to File First.

Two registrations protect two different things. When the budget covers one filing, the choice has a usual answer and a short list of honest exceptions.

By Nicholas K. Harris, Esq. · September 2026

One application, one mark

Almost every brand is two assets wearing one name: the name itself, and the design that carries it into the world. The USPTO does not register brands. It registers marks, one per application, and every application must present its mark in exactly one form. An owner who wants the name and the logo both protected files two applications, and each one carries its own government filing fee in each class of goods or services.

So the first budgeting question of nearly every new engagement is the same: word mark or logo, which to file first? The answer has a strong default and a set of genuine exceptions, and both come straight from how the two filing types work. Start with the mechanics, because the strategy is downstream of them.

Standard character and special form drawings

Under 37 C.F.R. 2.52, a trademark application presents its mark in one of two drawing forms.

A standard character drawing claims words, letters, or numbers, with no claim to any particular font, style, size, or color. This is the word mark. The drawing is simply the characters, typed. What the applicant claims is the wording itself, not any way of displaying it.

A special form drawing claims everything else: stylized lettering, a design, a logo, or a composite of wording and design together. The drawing is an image, and the image is the mark. Color may be claimed or not. A special form mark filed in black and white, without a color claim, covers the design in any color; a claimed color becomes part of the mark itself and narrows the claim to that presentation.

The drawing is not paperwork. It defines the property. Examination, the scope of the registration, what the owner can stop, and what the owner must keep using to maintain the registration all flow from what the drawing shows.

What each registration protects

A standard character registration protects the wording in every reasonable presentation. The Federal Circuit made the point explicit in In re Viterra: a standard character mark is not limited to any font, size, style, or color, so the registration reaches the wording in any lettering, size, or color. Rebrand the typography, invert the colors, hand the name to a new design agency, and the registration is untouched.

A special form registration protects the mark as depicted. The wording inside a logo still carries weight in any likelihood of confusion analysis, because consumers say and remember words, but the registered property is that presentation. The protection concentrates on the visual: the emblem, the arrangement, the stylization.

The common mistake is assuming that filing the logo "includes" the name, so that one filing covers both assets. It includes the name only as shown. A competitor who takes the name and dresses it differently sits squarely inside the reach of a word mark registration and only arguably inside the reach of a design registration. On the day enforcement matters, that difference can be the whole case.

Neither form is stronger in the abstract. They aim at different targets. The word mark polices the name; the design mark polices the look. The strategic question is which target matters first for this brand.

Why the word mark usually comes first

On a limited budget, the standard character filing is the usual first move, for reasons that compound:

  • Coverage per dollar. One standard character registration covers the name in every styling the brand will ever use. A design registration covers one presentation. When a single filing has to do the most work, the word mark does it.
  • Names outlast logos. Companies redesign their visual identity on a cycle and change their names rarely and painfully. The registration most worth owning is the one still accurate after the third redesign.
  • Infringement usually arrives as wording. The sound-alike product name, the copycat listing, the confusingly similar application that surfaces on the register: most real disputes turn on the name, not the art. The word mark is the right tool for the problems that actually occur.
  • Marketplaces read words. Amazon's Brand Registry lists two eligible mark types: a word mark, or a design mark containing words, letters, or numbers. A logo with no readable wording is not on that list. For sellers, the word mark is the key that turns the lock.

None of this makes the logo filing wrong. It makes it second in a sequence. When the budget covers both from the start, filing both is the complete answer, and the two applications proceed independently through examination. When the logo should go first

The default flips in a few recognizable situations.

When the name alone cannot get onto the Principal Register. A name that merely describes the goods, or that is primarily a surname, draws a refusal under Section 2(e) when filed as a word mark. The same wording inside a distinctive logo can register, with the unregistrable wording disclaimed, because the design supplies the distinctiveness the words lack. Owners in this position file the logo first because it is the filing that can succeed. What those refusals look like, and how they are answered, is the subject of our Office Action response work.

When the brand is design-led. Some marks are recognized by the eye before the name is read at all: an app icon, a badge on a garment, a character, an emblem on the shelf. If customers identify the source by the visual, and if counterfeiters copy the visual, the design registration protects the asset that is actually at risk.

When the field is crowded. In categories where the register is thick with similar wording, a name can be weak and hard to enforce even when it is registrable. Distinctiveness then lives in the presentation, and the design filing carries more of the practical protection than the word filing would.

Refiling as the brand evolves

Registrations are rigid in a way brands are not. A registered mark can be amended only within narrow limits: under 37 C.F.R. 2.72 and 2.173, no amendment may materially alter the mark. A genuine redesign, with a new emblem or new lettering or a new composition, is a different mark in the law's eyes, and it needs a new application with a new filing date. The old registration continues to protect the old look only for as long as the old look remains in use.

That rule has teeth at maintenance time. Keeping a registration alive requires periodic filings proving the mark is still in use, supported by specimens showing the mark as registered. A design registration for a logo the company shelved two redesigns ago cannot survive that proof. A standard character registration sails through every redesign, because the name in use is the name registered, whatever it happens to look like this year.

The practical sequence follows from all of it. File the word mark early and keep it for the life of the brand. File the logo once the visual identity is stable enough to deserve a registration of its own. After a real redesign, file the new form promptly, keep the old registration alive through the transition while legacy packaging and materials remain in commerce, and then let it lapse deliberately rather than paying to maintain a museum piece. Each new design filing takes its own place in line with its own priority date, which is one more argument for anchoring the name first: the word mark's date is the one that stays old.
