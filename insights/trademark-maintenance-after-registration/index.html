After Registration: Keeping a U.S. Trademark Alive.

The certificate is not the finish line. A federal registration survives only for owners who keep proving, on the statute's schedule, that the mark is still at work.

By Nicholas K. Harris, Esq. · September 2026

The register is rented, not owned

A United States trademark registration is not a deed. It is a living record that the law keeps alive only for owners who keep proving they deserve it. The Trademark Act conditions the survival of every registration on periodic filings, made on a statutory schedule, showing that the mark remains in use in commerce for the goods and services listed. Miss the schedule and the registration is cancelled or expires by operation of law. There is no hearing, no warning from an examiner, and no argument that restores what lapsed.

The logic is older than the paperwork. American trademark rights flow from use, not from registration, and the register exists to tell the public which marks are actually functioning in the marketplace. Congress and the USPTO prune it deliberately, clearing away deadwood so that new applicants are not blocked by registrations covering products nobody sells anymore. Every maintenance filing is a small trial of one question: is this mark still doing its job?

That is the frame in which the U.S. trademark maintenance and renewal requirements make sense. They are not a fee trap and not a formality. They are the recurring price of holding a public claim on a word or a design, and they run for as long as the owner wants the registration to run. Kept current, a registration can outlive the company that filed it. Neglected once, it is gone.

The filings that keep a registration alive

The Trademark Act names each filing by section, and the names are worth learning, because every USPTO record, reminder, and docket entry uses them.

  • The Section 8 declaration of use. A sworn statement under 15 U.S.C. § 1058 that the mark is in use in commerce for the goods and services in the registration, supported by a specimen showing the mark the way customers actually encounter it. Where use has lawfully paused, the statute accepts a declaration of excusable nonuse instead, with its own showing to make and its own scrutiny to survive.
  • The Section 9 renewal. An application under 15 U.S.C. § 1059 to renew the registration itself, filed on the recurring cycle the statute sets. In practice it travels together with a Section 8 declaration as a combined filing, and the cycle repeats for the life of the mark.
  • The Section 71 declaration, for Madrid registrations. An extension of protection to the United States under the Madrid Protocol lives on two calendars. The international registration renews centrally through WIPO, but the U.S. extension survives only through declarations of use filed at the USPTO under Section 71 of the Act. Holders who renew at WIPO and stop there lose the U.S. right, and they are often the last to find out.
  • The Section 15 declaration of incontestability. The one optional filing in the set. It does not preserve the registration; it strengthens it, and it is discussed on its own below.

Each required filing has a window fixed by statute and rule, and a grace period after it, available at the cost of an additional government fee. The windows are published, mechanical, and unforgiving. When the last one closes, the registration is dead, and the only road back to the Principal Register is a new application through the full examination process, with a new filing date, behind every application filed in the meantime. Rights that took years to build queue up behind strangers.

Incontestability, the filing nobody requires

Section 15 of the Act offers registrants something the required filings do not: an upgrade. Once the mark has been in continuous use in commerce for the period the statute prescribes, and while no adverse decision or pending proceeding stands against the owner's claim, the owner may file a declaration of incontestability.

The effect is substantial. An incontestable registration is conclusive evidence of the validity of the mark, of the registration, of the owner's ownership, and of the exclusive right to use the mark in commerce for the covered goods, subject only to the defenses the statute enumerates. The most common attack on a registered mark, the argument that it is merely descriptive and should never have registered, is cut off. In litigation, that difference can decide a case before discovery begins.

Because it is optional, incontestability is the filing most often skipped, usually by owners who never learned it existed. It costs a declaration and a modest government fee, and it is routinely made alongside the first required maintenance filing, one signature covering both. For a mark the business intends to keep, there is rarely a good reason to leave it on the table.

The audit program: the USPTO checks the homework

The USPTO runs a post-registration audit program, permanent since 2017, and it is neither a rumor nor a rare event. Maintenance filings that claim use across multiple goods or services are selected at random, and the selected owner receives an office action requiring proof of use for additional items beyond the ones the filed specimen covered.

The proof must be real. Photographs of the goods, packaging, labels, screenshots of live commerce, whatever shows the mark actually in use for each queried item. Goods that cannot be supported are deleted from the registration, and once an audit has issued, deletions carry a government fee of their own. Fail to respond at all and the entire registration is cancelled, not just the audited goods.

The program exists because registers fill with claims that outlive reality. Products get discontinued, lines get narrowed, and the registration keeps listing everything from the optimistic first filing. The clean practice is to delete what is no longer in use before signing the declaration, not after the Office asks. A maintenance declaration is a sworn statement, and a signature covering goods the owner knows are out of use invites consequences far worse than a trimmed registration, up to attacks on the registration as a whole.

An audit response is an office action response, with a docketed deadline and a record that follows the registration for life. It deserves the same discipline as an examination-stage office action response: answer once, completely, with evidence, and concede what the evidence will not carry.

Why the calendar, and counsel, still matter

Everything above reduces to dates and sworn statements, which is exactly how registrations die. The USPTO sends courtesy reminders to the email address of record, and only there. Companies change domains, the employee who filed moves on, the founder's inbox from the filing year goes unread. No reminder follows the owner around; the obligation does.

The docket has to outlive the people who made it. A registration's maintenance dates span decades. A calendar entry on one person's laptop is not a docket; a system that survives turnover, with the windows and the grace periods both entered, is.

The record has to stay current. Assignments belong on record with the USPTO, name changes belong in the file, and the correspondence address has to point somewhere a human reads. A chain of title that stops two owners ago turns a routine filing into a research project on a deadline.

The scams have to be filtered. Registration records are public, and an industry of look-alike outfits mails official-seeming notices quoting invented deadlines and inflated charges. With counsel of record, genuine USPTO correspondence goes to the attorney, and anything that arrives by another route can be read with the suspicion it has earned.

The specimens have to be honest and current. Packaging is redesigned, websites are rebuilt, and the specimen that supported the original application may show a version of the mark or a product the company retired years ago. Someone has to look before someone signs.

This is why post-registration work belongs on a professional's docket rather than in a drawer. It is quiet work: nothing happens for years, then a window opens, and everything turns on a sworn document being right the first time.
