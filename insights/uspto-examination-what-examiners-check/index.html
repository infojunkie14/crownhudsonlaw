What USPTO Examining Attorneys Actually Check.

Registration is decided by a government lawyer with a search system and a checklist. Here is what actually happens to an application on that desk.

By Nicholas K. Harris, Esq. · September 2026

The lawyer on the other side of the filing

A federal trademark application is not reviewed by a clerk or scored by software. It is assigned to an examining attorney: a lawyer employed by the USPTO, working in one of the Office's law offices, whose job is to decide whether the mark is entitled to a place on the federal register. Examination is ex parte, which means nobody argues the other side. The examiner is investigator and judge in the same chair, bound by the Trademark Act, by the Office's rules, and by the Trademark Manual of Examining Procedure, the TMEP, which scripts nearly every move an examiner is permitted to make.

Applications are taken up roughly in the order they were filed. If the file raises no issues, the examiner approves the mark for publication, and the applicant may never learn how much scrutiny it quietly survived. If the file raises issues, every one of them goes into an Office Action, because the examiner is expected to state all grounds in the first action rather than mete them out one letter at a time. The USPTO trademark examination process is, in that sense, a single comprehensive audit run to a strict script, and it rewards applications drafted by someone who has read the script.

The search the examiner runs first

The first substantive task is the conflict search, because likelihood of confusion under Section 2(d) of the Trademark Act is a ground the examiner must raise on the Office's own initiative. Owners of existing registrations do not police the register at this stage; the government does it for them. And the search is nothing like typing the mark into a search box.

Examiners search the federal register through the Office's own system, with operators built for the ways trademarks actually collide:

  • Truncation and pattern matching, so that QUICK, QUIK, KWIK, and KWIX surface as one family rather than four unrelated words.
  • Phonetic equivalents and letter substitutions, because confusion is judged by sound and overall impression as well as spelling.
  • Foreign translations. Under the doctrine of foreign equivalents, non-English wording is translated and searched against its English meaning, so LUPO can collide with WOLF.
  • Design codes for logos. Figurative elements are indexed under a numerical classification, so two stylized mountain peaks can conflict without sharing a single letter.
  • Related goods and services, not just the filed class. The Office's coordinated class groupings exist because confusion routinely crosses class lines, and the examiner searches accordingly.

The search also sweeps earlier-filed applications that have not yet registered. A pending application cannot support a refusal by itself, so the examiner flags it, and once the rest of the file is in order the later application is suspended to wait on the earlier one; if the earlier mark registers, the refusal follows. A serious pre-filing knockout search mirrors this whole exercise, and that is the entire argument for running one: the conflicts an examiner will find are findable in advance, when walking away costs almost nothing. That search is the first step of our U.S. trademark registration service, and it ends a meaningful share of proposed marks before anything is filed.

The refusal checklist

With the search done, the examiner works through the rest of the file in a sequence the TMEP effectively dictates. Five clusters of issues account for nearly everything that appears in Office Actions.

  • Conflicts. Section 2(d) refusals built on the search above: one or more cited registrations, an analysis of the marks' similarity in sound, appearance, and commercial impression, and evidence that the goods or services are related. This is the refusal with the highest stakes and the one where the quality of the eventual response matters most.
  • Descriptiveness and its relatives. Section 2(e)(1) refusals for marks that immediately describe the goods, plus the neighboring grounds: primarily geographically descriptive terms, primarily merely surnames, wording that fails to function as a mark at all, and ornamental slogans that decorate a product rather than identify its source.
  • Specimens. In use-based filings the examiner inspects the proof of use like a skeptical customer: does it show this mark, on these goods or in the sale or advertising of these services, working as a trademark? Printer's proofs, digital mockups, and images that look fabricated are refused, and examiners are trained to spot them.
  • Identifications and classification. The goods and services must be identified with definiteness and placed in the correct classes. Vague wording, catchall phrases, and boilerplate borrowed from someone else's registration all draw requirements. Wording taken from the Office's own ID Manual generally does not, because those entries are pre-approved.
  • Procedural and record requirements. Entity type and citizenship, a proper domicile address, U.S. counsel for foreign-domiciled applicants under 37 C.F.R. 2.11, disclaimers of unregistrable components, translations and transliterations of non-English wording, a description of the mark for stylized filings, and written consent where the mark names a living person.

None of this is discretionary. If the record raises an issue, the examiner is obligated to act on it. That points at the honest way to reduce refusals: not persuasion after the fact, but a record that raises fewer issues in the first place.

How conversations with the examiner actually work

Examination is a written process, but it is not a silent one, and the informal channel is one of the most underused assets in trademark prosecution. Three kinds of contact matter.

The examiner's amendment. When the only problems in a file can be fixed without argument (a tightened identification, a disclaimer, a corrected entity type), examiners are encouraged to resolve them directly with the attorney of record instead of issuing a formal Office Action. A short exchange, an authorization from counsel, and the examiner enters the change personally; the application moves toward publication without a response cycle at all. It is the fastest good outcome in the whole process, and in practice it happens when the examiner can reach someone who immediately understands what is being asked and has authority to agree to it.

Contact after a refusal. Once an Office Action issues, counsel can ask the examiner what would actually resolve it. Examiners will often say, in plain terms, which amendment would satisfy a requirement, or whether any narrowing of the goods would clear a conflict. What they will not do is negotiate the law: an examiner who believes two marks are confusingly similar is moved by evidence and argument on the record, not by conversation. Knowing which kind of issue you are holding, one that can be talked through or one that must be briefed, is much of what an experienced practitioner is for, and it shapes everything about how we build an Office Action response.

The record itself. Whatever happens informally, decisions live in the written file, and the file is public. Once an attorney appears, the Office corresponds with the attorney alone. Every argument, amendment, and examiner note becomes part of a permanent history that a future examiner, or a future adversary, can read, so prosecution is conducted with the awareness that the file is forever.

Why careful drafting shrinks the refusal surface

Every field of an application is a surface examination can catch on: the mark, the drawing, the identification, the class, the filing basis, the specimen, the entity, the address. Most requirements and a good share of refusals are not bad luck. They are drafting choices, visible in advance to anyone who knows the checklist above.

Professional drafting works that checklist in reverse. The identification is built from the ID Manual's pre-approved wording, so definiteness requirements never arise, and it is scoped to what the client actually sells, so the application is not carrying conflict exposure it gets nothing for. The specimen is chosen before filing and inspected against the drawing and the listed goods the way the examiner will inspect it. Disclaimers that are plainly required are volunteered. The choice of drawing, standard characters or a stylized form, is made deliberately, because it fixes the scope of the registration for its whole life. Entity, citizenship, and domicile are stated correctly the first time.

The economics follow directly. A clean application can be approved on first review, and each defect buys at least one more round of prosecution with attorney time attached. That arithmetic is why this firm charges flat fees, stated in writing, for the whole job rather than billing the hours a defective filing generates: the incentive to file it right the first time is ours as much as the client's. The fees sit on the pricing page for anyone to read before an engagement is signed.

If you are deciding whether to file, or you would like to know what an examiner is likely to make of a mark you already use, ask. Write to the firm with the mark and a description of the goods or services, and you will get a plain answer about what examination is likely to raise and whether filing is worth it.
