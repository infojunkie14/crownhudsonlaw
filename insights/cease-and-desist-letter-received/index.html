You Received a Trademark Cease and Desist. Read This Before Replying.

The letter is built to frighten you into a fast answer. The fast answer is almost always the wrong one.

By Nicholas K. Harris, Esq. · September 2026

What a cease and desist letter is, and what it is not

A trademark cease and desist letter is a private demand. Someone believes your business name, product name, logo, or slogan collides with rights they hold, and they, or more often their lawyer, have written to tell you so. The letter typically asserts a registration or a history of use, accuses your use of infringing it, and demands that you stop. It may demand more: destruction of inventory, transfer of a domain name, a written promise never to resume, sometimes an accounting of the money you made under the mark.

Here is what it is not. It is not a lawsuit. Nothing has been filed, no court has looked at the claim, and no one has decided that you infringe. It is an opening position, drafted by an advocate whose job is to make the sender's case sound finished. The response date printed in bold near the end was chosen by the person who wrote the letter. It is not a court deadline and it carries no legal force of its own.

It is also not nothing. The letter puts you on notice of the sender's claim, and what you do after notice becomes part of the story. If the dispute ever reaches a courtroom, continuing exactly as before, with full knowledge of the claim, is the raw material of an argument that your conduct was willful. A demand letter is frequently the cheapest step in an escalation the sender has already mapped out. So the letter deserves neither terror nor contempt. It deserves an assessment.

The two reflexes, and why both go wrong

Knowing which letter you are holding is a judgment, not a mood, and it comes out of the assessment below.

Nearly everyone who receives one of these letters reaches for one of two responses within the hour. Both are mistakes.

The panic reply. A frightened recipient writes back the same day: we had no idea, we are so sorry, we will sell through the stock we have and then stop. Every sentence of that email is evidence. The apology reads as an admission that the sender's rights are valid and that you crossed them. The promise creates a commitment, and if business reality later makes you break it, you have handed the other side something worse than the original dispute. The angry version is no better; a hostile reply gets attached as an exhibit to show bad faith. Anything you send can be quoted back to you, and the sender's lawyer is reading your reply with exactly that in mind.

The drawer. The opposite reflex is to decide the letter is a bluff and file it where letters go to die. Sometimes it is a bluff. But when the sender is serious, silence does not make the claim smaller; it makes your position worse, because the clock on your after-notice conduct is already running. And modern enforcement rarely goes straight to federal court, where the sender bears real cost. It goes through cheaper doors first: a takedown complaint to Amazon or Etsy that suspends your listings, an app store complaint, a domain dispute, an opposition or cancellation proceeding against your own application or registration at the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board. Ignoring the letter forfeits the stage where the dispute is cheapest to shape.

There are letters that can safely receive no reply. Knowing which letter you are holding is a judgment, not a mood, and it comes out of the assessment below.

The assessment that should happen before any reply

Three questions decide everything that follows: what they have, what you have, and what is at stake. In practice that means working through a short list before a single word goes back to the sender.

  • Verify their rights. If the letter cites a federal registration, pull it in the USPTO's public TSDR database. Is it live? Does the sender actually own it? What goods and services does it really cover? Demand letters routinely describe a registration as broader than it is, and a claim built on a narrow or half-dead registration reads very differently once you have the record in front of you.
  • Weigh the mark's strength. An invented word with years of heavy use is a strong mark. A descriptive phrase that a dozen competitors also use is a weak one, and weak marks earn narrow protection. Some letters assert near-generic terms and depend entirely on the recipient not knowing that.
  • Establish your own position. When did you first use your mark, and where? Do you hold a registration or a pending application of your own? Priority of use matters, and it occasionally turns out that the recipient of the letter is the senior party.
  • Test the actual overlap. Infringement under Sections 32 and 43(a) of the Lanham Act turns on likelihood of confusion, a multi-factor judgment about the marks, the goods, the trade channels, and the customers. Similar names on genuinely unrelated goods, sold in different channels to different buyers, are often not a legal problem at all.
  • Price the exposure honestly. What does the name earn you, and what would changing it cost in packaging, inventory, domains, and accumulated search equity? Against that, how plausible is the sender's damages theory? The economics of the dispute, not just the merits, should drive the response.
  • Consider the sender. A letter from a brand with a documented enforcement history is a different object than boilerplate generated by a watch service, and different again from an overreaching claimant asserting a descriptive term against half an industry.

One more branch of the assessment cuts the other way: their registration may be vulnerable. Registrations can be attacked for nonuse, for fraud in procurement, for genericness. A recipient who can credibly threaten a cancellation proceeding is negotiating from a very different chair.

The response postures, from pushback to rebrand

Once the assessment is done, the realistic responses sit on a spectrum, and the right point on it is a strategic choice rather than a moral one.

Substantive refusal. When the claim is weak, the answer is a reasoned letter saying no: the marks differ, the goods do not overlap, your use is senior, their registration does not reach what they say it reaches. A well-built refusal often ends the matter, because it tells the sender that the cheap win is off the table and anything further will cost them real money.

Questions and proof. A measured reply can ask the sender to substantiate the claim: specimens of their use, dates, the basis for the alleged confusion. It concedes nothing, buys time legitimately, and frequently exposes how thin the file behind the letter is.

Coexistence. Many disputes end in an agreement that divides the territory: you stay in your goods and channels, they stay in theirs, each side consents to the other's registration within those lanes. Done well, a coexistence agreement converts a threat into certainty for both parties.

Modification. Sometimes a modest change dissolves the conflict: adding your house brand to the disputed product name, adjusting a logo, narrowing how and where the mark is used. The sender gets distance between the marks; you keep most of what you built.

Negotiated exit. When the sender is right but rigid timing would hurt you, the deal to seek is a phase-out: a transition on a schedule you can live with, sell-through of existing inventory, no payment or a token one, mutual releases.

Rebrand. When the sender's rights are real, senior, and squarely on point, the honest advice is to change the name, and to treat the moment as the correction of an old mistake rather than a defeat. The businesses that end up in this position almost always got there by adopting a name no one ever cleared. The budget that would have gone to a losing fight is better spent on a name that searches clean and on registering it properly, so that the next demand letter in your life is one you send.

Why the reply should go through counsel

Plenty of founders draft their own response, and some get away with it. The reasons not to are structural, not ceremonial.

First, your reply is evidence, and an attorney writes it knowing that. A response drafted for settlement is framed so that concessions made in negotiation stay in negotiation. A response drafted at midnight by the business owner tends to admit facts, adopt the sender's characterizations, and volunteer information no one asked for.

Second, the core judgment is legal. Likelihood of confusion is a doctrine with decades of case law behind each factor, and lay assessments miss in both directions: some recipients capitulate to letters a first-year associate would have shrugged off, and others confidently ignore claims that were, in fact, meritorious and cheap to settle early. The expensive outcomes on both ends come from misjudging the merits, and the merits are the part a trademark lawyer reads quickly.

Third, a letter that arrives on firm letterhead changes the sender's arithmetic. It signals that the cheap-intimidation phase is over and that escalation will be contested. Senders behave differently when the recipient is represented; it is one of the oldest facts of practice.
