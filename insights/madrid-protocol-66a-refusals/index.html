Madrid Protocol Refusals in the United States: What 66(a) Holders Should Know

A provisional refusal from the USPTO is not the end of a United States designation; it is the start of a United States prosecution, with its own rules and its own clock.

By Nicholas K. Harris, Esq. · September 2026

A refusal that arrives by way of Geneva

When an international registration under the Madrid Protocol designates the United States, WIPO transmits the request to the USPTO, where it becomes an application under Section 66(a) of the Trademark Act: a request for an extension of protection, examined under United States law. If the examining attorney finds a problem, the objection travels back through Geneva as a notification of provisional refusal. For many holders, that notification is the first sign that the United States designation will not simply sail through.

A Madrid Protocol provisional refusal in the United States is common, it is usually answerable, and it runs on strict, short deadlines. This article explains how the USPTO examines a 66(a) designation, why so many draw refusals, and what a holder outside the United States needs to do about one.

How the USPTO examines a Section 66(a) designation

The USPTO does not treat a Madrid designation as a courtesy to be waved along. A 66(a) application receives the same substantive examination as a domestic filing. The examining attorney searches the United States register and refuses under Section 2(d) where the mark is confusingly similar to an earlier registration or pending application. Marks are refused under Section 2(e) as merely descriptive, deceptively misdescriptive, geographically descriptive, or primarily a surname. The examiner may require disclaimers of unregistrable wording, English translations or transliterations of foreign words, a clearer description of the mark, and a good deal else.

A few features are specific to the Madrid route. The application arrives with a declaration of intention to use the mark in United States commerce, so no specimen of use is required before registration issues. The classification assigned by the International Bureau is fixed: goods cannot be moved between classes and classes cannot be added, however sensible the reshuffle would be under USPTO practice. And the identification of goods and services can be narrowed, but never broadened beyond the scope of the international registration.

Why provisional refusals are so common: the identification gap

The single largest generator of provisional refusals is the distance between what WIPO will accept in an identification of goods and services and what the USPTO demands.

Most of the world drafts identifications broadly. Class headings and sweeping terms ("clothing," "computer software," "advertising services") pass without comment in many national offices and at the International Bureau. United States practice runs the other way: the USPTO requires identifications definite enough that an ordinary reader knows exactly what the goods are, and it polices that standard through its Acceptable Identification of Goods and Services Manual. "Computer software" must say what the software does. "Clothing" must name the garments.

Because international registrations are usually drafted to the broad standard, a large share of United States designations draw at least one refusal on this ground alone. Alongside the identification requirement, the grounds that appear most often in Madrid-based refusals are:

  • Indefinite identifications: broad or vague wording that must be redrafted to USPTO standards without exceeding the scope of the international registration.
  • Section 2(d) likelihood of confusion: an earlier United States registration or application that the home-country clearance search never looked at.
  • Section 2(e) refusals: descriptiveness measured in English, which can surprise a holder whose mark is fanciful in its home language.
  • Disclaimer and translation requirements: unregistrable wording to be disclaimed, foreign wording to be translated or transliterated.
  • The United States counsel requirement itself, discussed next, which appears in nearly every provisional refusal issued to a foreign-domiciled holder.

None of this reflects a weak mark. It reflects a national office with its own statute, its own crowded register, and the most particular identification practice in the world.

The United States counsel requirement

Since 2019, every trademark applicant, registrant, or party to a proceeding who is domiciled outside the United States must be represented before the USPTO by an attorney licensed in a United States jurisdiction. The rule is 37 C.F.R. 2.11, and it applies with full force to Madrid holders. The representative recorded at WIPO, however capable, cannot file a response, sign an amendment, or argue with the examining attorney unless that person also happens to hold a United States law license.

In practice a provisional refusal therefore does two things at once: it raises the substantive objections, and it obliges the holder to appoint United States counsel before anything can be filed in reply. The appointment is not a formality to defer until the substance is sorted out; nothing moves until it is made. Answering these refusals for foreign holders and their home-country firms is a core part of this practice, described at Madrid Protocol refusals.

How a response works

The refusal arrives through WIPO, but the answer does not go back the same way. A response is filed directly with the USPTO through its electronic filing system, by United States counsel, and from that point the file runs like any other United States prosecution.

A sound response takes each ground on its own terms. Identification objections are usually resolved by amendment: redrafting the goods and services into acceptable language while giving up as little commercial scope as possible. Section 2(d) refusals are argued on the record, comparing the marks, the goods, the trade channels, and the realities of the marketplace; sometimes a consent agreement with the owner of the cited registration is the practical path. Descriptiveness refusals may be argued, or met with a claim of acquired distinctiveness where the record supports one. Disclaimers and translations are simply entered where resisting them would serve no purpose. Part of the judgment is knowing which requirements to satisfy quickly and which refusals deserve a fight, the same triage we apply to every Office Action response.

The deadlines are strict and short, and they run from the date the USPTO issued the refusal, not from the day the holder learned of it. The notification's trip through Geneva, and often through a home-country firm's docket, can quietly consume much of the available time. A designation that misses its deadline is abandoned in the United States. The international registration survives and the other designations are untouched, but the United States filing date, and any priority attached to it, is gone. If the examining attorney maintains the refusal after a response, a final refusal follows, and the holder may appeal to the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board.

Working with the home-country representative

A Madrid refusal creates a triangle: the holder, the representative who manages the international registration, and United States counsel. These matters run best when the triangle is explicit about who does what.

The home representative keeps the international registration itself: renewals, recordals of ownership changes, and the health of the basic mark on which the registration depends during its early years. United States counsel takes the USPTO file: the response, the deadline, all correspondence with the examining attorney, and the strategic questions that are really questions of United States law. The holder, usually through the home firm, supplies what the response needs: how the mark is used or will be used in the United States market, which goods actually matter commercially, and instructions on the tradeoffs when scope must be narrowed.

Two points of coordination deserve particular care. First, narrowing the identification in the United States affects only the United States; the international registration and every other designation keep their original scope, so a holder should not fight to preserve wording it does not commercially need here. Second, if the basic mark comes under attack at home while the United States designation is pending, United States counsel should hear about it promptly, because the fate of the international registration can carry the designation down with it, and transformation into a national application is a decision with its own clock.

What to do when the notification arrives

Read the refusal in full, including the pages that look like boilerplate; the operative requirements are often scattered through them. Calendar the deadline from the USPTO issue date immediately, not from the day the notification reached you. Engage United States counsel early enough that the response can be drafted with time to think, and send counsel the complete notification together with the particulars of the international registration. Most provisional refusals are answerable, and many are answerable well, but not from a standing start in the final days.
