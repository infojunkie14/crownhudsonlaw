The Supplemental Register: Consolation Prize or Smart Move?

The USPTO keeps a second list. Knowing what it buys, and what it quietly costs, turns a descriptiveness refusal from a dead end into a decision.

By Nicholas K. Harris, Esq. · September 2026

Contents

  2. What a Supplemental registration actually buys
  3. What it does not buy
  4. When amending beats fighting the refusal
  5. The road back to the Principal Register

Two registers, one statute

The federal trademark system keeps two lists. The Principal Register is the one everybody means when they say "registered trademark": the register that carries the legal presumptions, the constructive notice, the path to incontestability. The Supplemental Register, created by the Trademark Act of 1946 and governed by Sections 23 through 28 of the Act, 15 U.S.C. 1091 through 1096, is the second list, reserved for marks that are capable of distinguishing goods or services but do not yet do so in the eyes of the law.

In practice that means merely descriptive terms, surnames, geographically descriptive wording, and certain product designs and configurations. A term like SPEEDY DELIVERY for a courier tells the buyer what the service is, not who provides it, so it cannot sit on the Principal Register without proof that consumers have come to see it as a brand. It can, however, sit on the Supplemental Register while it earns that status. Generic terms qualify for neither register, ever, and no amount of use changes that.

Two eligibility rules trip people up. First, a mark must actually be in use in commerce to register on the Supplemental Register; an intent-to-use application cannot amend over until use is alleged, and making the switch moves the application's effective filing date to the date use was alleged, which surrenders the original date's priority. Second, an application that reached the USPTO as a Madrid Protocol designation under Section 66(a) is barred from the Supplemental Register entirely by Section 68(a)(4). A foreign holder refused on descriptiveness has a different set of options, which is a recurring part of our Madrid Protocol refusal work.

What a Supplemental registration actually buys

Nothing about accepting the Supplemental Register waives a future application.

The reputation of the Supplemental Register as a booby prize is undeserved, because its most valuable feature is invisible until you sit where an examining attorney sits. A Supplemental registration is a federal registration. It goes into the USPTO's database, and examining attorneys cite Supplemental registrations under Section 2(d) to refuse later applications for confusingly similar marks, exactly as they cite Principal ones. Your descriptive mark, registered, becomes a wall that later filers run into during examination, without you lifting a finger or paying a litigator.

The rest of the bundle is real too. The owner may use the federal registration symbol, the circled R. The registration supports a lawsuit in federal court under the Lanham Act. It appears in every clearance search a competent attorney runs, and cautious counsel steer clients away from names that collide with it. It can serve as a home-country registration supporting filings abroad. And it holds the mark's place in the federal system while the brand does the slow work of becoming distinctive through sales, advertising, and time.

What it does not buy

Section 26 of the Act strips the Supplemental Register of the advantages that make the Principal Register the prize. A Supplemental registration confers:

  • No presumption of validity or ownership. The Section 7(b) presumptions, validity, ownership, and the exclusive right to use the mark, belong to Principal registrations only. In court, a Supplemental registrant must still prove the mark has become distinctive before any infringement question is even reached.
  • No constructive notice and no nationwide priority. The Section 22 notice and Section 7(c) constructive-use rights that let a Principal registration cut off innocent later adopters across the country do not attach.
  • No incontestability, ever. A Supplemental registration can never mature into incontestable status under Section 15, no matter how long it lives.
  • No border enforcement. It cannot be recorded with U.S. Customs and Border Protection to block infringing imports at the port.
  • No opposition period, and thinner armor. Supplemental applications are not published for opposition; they simply register, and they remain open to cancellation without some of the repose a seasoned Principal registration enjoys.

One more cost is strategic rather than statutory. Accepting the Supplemental Register is generally treated as a concession that the mark was not inherently distinctive at the time. That admission can be quoted back at you in a later dispute. It does not say the mark can never become distinctive, and it does not poison a future claim that it has, but it is on the record, and the decision to put it there should be made knowingly.

When amending beats fighting the refusal

The Supplemental Register usually enters the conversation when a Section 2(e)(1) descriptiveness refusal arrives in an Office Action. At that point there are three honest paths: argue that the mark is suggestive rather than descriptive, prove acquired distinctiveness under Section 2(f), or amend to the Supplemental Register. The anatomy of that letter is described on our Office Action response page; choosing among the three paths is the whole job.

Arguing makes sense when the examiner is genuinely reachable. The line between descriptive and suggestive is famously blurry, and a mark that requires a mental leap to connect to the goods can be defended on the record. But when the term plainly describes the product, when the examiner's dictionary and internet evidence is on point, and when your own website uses the wording descriptively, the argument is a paid trip to a final refusal.

Amending tends to be the smarter move when several things are true at once. The refusal is well founded, so the fight is a bad bet. The brand is young, so there is no Section 2(f) evidence to offer yet. And the client's real need is near-term and practical: the registration symbol, a listing in the database that blocks copycats at the examination stage, a registration certificate that platforms and partners ask to see. Amendment typically resolves the descriptiveness refusal without months of argument, and it preserves every right to come back for the Principal Register later. Nothing about accepting the Supplemental Register waives a future application.

Cost belongs in that calculus. An argued response is bespoke work; an amendment is usually short work, and the fee should reflect the difference. Ours are flat fees, stated in writing on the pricing page, and the recommendation comes before the engagement rather than after.

The road back to the Principal Register

A Supplemental registration is not a life sentence, but there is no conversion lever either. The route back is a new application seeking the Principal Register, filed whenever the mark can carry a claim of acquired distinctiveness under Section 2(f). The Supplemental registration stays alive in the meantime, doing its blocking work, provided its own maintenance filings are kept up.

Acquired distinctiveness is proved the unglamorous way: evidence that consumers have come to treat the wording as a brand. Sales figures and their growth. Advertising spend and, more importantly, advertising that uses the term as a mark rather than as a description. Unsolicited press. Declarations from customers and industry participants. Survey evidence in the hard cases. And the statute offers a shortcut worth knowing: under Section 2(f), the Office may accept proof of substantially exclusive and continuous use of the mark in commerce for the five years before the claim as prima facie evidence that it has become distinctive. Time on the Supplemental Register does not itself prove distinctiveness, but the use that accumulates during those years is exactly the record a Section 2(f) claim is built from.

The sequence, then, is a strategy rather than a defeat: register on the Supplemental Register now, build the brand deliberately, keep the evidence as you go, and file for the Principal Register when the record supports it. That second filing is an ordinary U.S. trademark application with a Section 2(f) claim attached, and owners who kept their sales and advertising records find it a far easier climb.

So, is it worth it?

Whether a Supplemental Register trademark is worth it comes down to what the alternative actually is. Measured against a Principal registration, it is plainly the lesser thing. Measured against the realistic alternatives, an abandoned application, a rebrand nobody budgeted for, or a fight the record says you lose, it is often the strongest value on offer: a federal registration, the symbol, examination-stage blocking power, and a held place in line, all for the price of a short amendment. The consolation-prize framing fails because it compares the Supplemental Register to something the descriptive mark could not have had anyway. The right comparison is to nothing, and it beats nothing comfortably.

If a descriptiveness refusal has arrived and you are weighing the fight against the amendment, ask. Write to the firm with the serial number or a copy of the Office Action, and you will get a plain answer about which of the three paths the record supports.
